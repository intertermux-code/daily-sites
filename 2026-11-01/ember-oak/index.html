01

The Wood Comes From an Orchard

Each week a truck arrives from a cider orchard in Hood River with trees that have stopped fruiting — thirty-year-old apple, plus oak felled in the Coast Range under permit. Nothing resinous ever crosses the threshold. We split, stack and season the wood on the patio, and the cook who lights the hearth decides how dry a stick is by the sound it makes when dropped.

02

The Cold Room Does the Slow Work

Primal cuts hang whole for six weeks behind glass at the bar, at 34 degrees and slow air. Enzymes soften the fibre, the surface dries to a crust we trim away each service, and what is left tastes faintly of walnut and beef stock reduced for hours. We age only what we can sell in ten days, so nothing turns.

03

Nobody Plans the Menu Before Noon

Chef Corin Vale writes the day on a whiteboard: what the fish market delivered, what the farm cut this morning, how the fire is behaving. By five the board is wiped and the tickets are printed. That is why the printed card lists cuts rather than promises — a wood fire has moods, and we cook with them instead of against them.

04

The Table Is the Last Course

Dinner here is meant to run three hours. Linen, low light, a cast-iron plate that stays hot through the last bite, and servers who know the difference between the two ribeyes on the pass. Come hungry, hand over the timing, and let the room do the rest.

The Craft

Ask the Kitchen Anything

Open a panel. Sommelier Nadia Reyes and Chef Vale answer the four questions asked at almost every service.

Gas gives heat; wood gives weather. A bed of oak embers radiates dry, uneven, living warmth — 700 degrees over the flat stone, 300 at the lip — and it lays a thin layer of smoke on everything it touches. We can steer a fire but never schedule it, which is exactly why the board gets written at noon rather than a month ahead.

We built a 40-square-foot still-air cabinet behind the bar and run it at 34°F with UV-sterilised circulation. Primals arrive from a herd in Eastern Washington, hang whole for four to six weeks, and get trimmed to order each morning. You can watch the room through the glass, and the day’s trim goes into the marrow butter.

Ember-leek toast to start, one 16-ounce ribeye between two, the ash-buried beets, and potato bread with marrow butter. Add the smoked old fashioned and let your server pair a Willamette Pinot by the glass. That is the house in four tickets, and it is what we would order ourselves.

Yes, with a day’s notice. The kitchen runs a separate ash grill for vegetable-only services, and eight of the sixteen menu items are already vegetarian without substitution. Tell us at booking and Chef Vale will write a version of the tasting menu for your table — no charge for the extra labour.
