The Quiet Collapse
of the Open Web

How three walled gardens came to host seventy-eight percent of the internet’s traffic, and why the engineers who built the decentralized web are packing up their servers.

By Elena Vasquez Senior Correspondent · Infrastructure Desk 24 min read · Published at 05:00 ET

The first time Miriam Okonkwo noticed that the web was shrinking, she was sitting in a rented cage in a data center in Suwanee, Georgia, watching a traceroute fail. It was 2023, and she was troubleshooting an open-source video archive that had been online, without interruption, for seventeen years. The packets left her rack, hopped three networks, and disappeared. Not dropped. Not filtered. Simply absorbed — rerouted, she would later learn, into a single content delivery network whose name she could not, at the time, pronounce correctly.

“I thought it was a transient,” she told me in August, over a phone line so clean it felt almost antique. “Then it happened again on a Tuesday. Then it happened every day for a week. By the end of the month, I’d lost four of my nine upstream peers, and none of them would return a support ticket.”

Okonkwo runs what is, by her own description, the last non-commercial archive of early-2000s independent web comics. It is 14.2 terabytes in size. It is hosted on a single 4U chassis that she bought refurbished in 2017. It does not use a content delivery network, and its monthly bandwidth bill, as of this writing, is $842. It is one of roughly six thousand independent hosts that Signal was able to identify across four continents in an eight-month investigation that ended last week.

What we found, in short, is this: the open web is not dying. It is being quietly foreclosed. The traffic that once flowed freely across tens of thousands of independent networks has pooled into three commercial gardens, whose gates are not locked but whose keys are rented by the month at rates calibrated to discourage casual visitors.

I. The Three Gardens

Begin with a number, and work outward. In 2014, according to an analysis we commissioned from the nonprofit measurement firm Caida, roughly 23 percent of consumer internet traffic on the North American backbone traversed one of three specific content delivery networks. By 2020, that figure had climbed to 47 percent. By the end of 2025, it stood at 78.3 percent.

The three networks — which we will refer to here by the abbreviations their customers use: A, G, and F — do not compete with one another in any traditional sense. They coexist, largely, by geographic convention. A dominates the American West and the Indian subcontinent. G holds the European Union and most of Africa. F runs the rest: East Asia, South America, and, somewhat surprisingly, the Eastern seaboard of the United States, where A’s pricing has long been considered uncompetitive.

“It is not a monopoly in the legal sense. It is a monopoly in the topological sense. The graph simply has fewer nodes.”

— Dr. Hana Lindqvist, Oxford Internet Institute

It is tempting to read this as a story about corporate ambition. It is more accurate to read it as a story about physics, economics, and the way that a sufficiently large advantage in latency compounds over time into an advantage in everything else. A millisecond saved is a customer retained. A customer retained is a log line generated. A log line generated is a model trained. A model trained is a millisecond saved.

The cycle is not vicious. It is circular. And it is remarkably difficult to exit once you have entered.

II. The Engineers Who Stay

There are, still, engineers who run independent servers. Signal spoke with forty-three of them over the course of this investigation. Their ages range from twenty-one to seventy-four. Their hardware ranges from a Raspberry Pi in a shoebox to a rack that would have cost $240,000 to buy new. Their motivations vary: ideological, archival, commercial, stubborn, sentimental, and, in one memorable case, tax-related.

What unites them is a shared frustration with the prevailing metaphor. The internet, they say, is not a cloud. It is not a platform. It is not a utility. It is, or was, a collection of agreements — mostly informal, occasionally contractual — between autonomous networks to carry one another’s traffic without charge. These agreements, known as peering, were the structural innovation that allowed the internet to scale without a central operator.

Over the last decade, peering has not been abolished. It has been priced. The major networks now require independent hosts to purchase what is called transit — a paid service — to reach their customers. The price per megabit, across the three major gardens, has dropped by roughly 88 percent since 2012. The price of not purchasing transit — in lost reachability, in failed requests, in invisible redirects — has risen by a factor that is harder to measure but easier to feel.

III. A Note on Methodology

Signal’s findings are based on three parallel efforts. First, a set of 1,200 traceroutes performed daily from twelve geographically distributed vantage points between February and September of this year. Second, a survey of 447 independent hosts, conducted in partnership with the nonprofit Electronic Frontier Foundation. Third, a review of 18,000 pages of public filings, peering agreements, and tariff documents obtained through freedom-of-information requests in six jurisdictions.

Our full methodology, along with the raw traceroute data and the survey instrument, is published separately at signal.press/methods/open-web-2026.

IV. What Is To Be Done

There are, broadly, three schools of thought among the engineers Signal interviewed about what might come next.

The first — call it the retreat school — holds that the independent web is already a lost cause, and that the most honorable course is to migrate one’s archives to a major platform and negotiate the best possible terms. This view is most popular among archivists in their fifties and sixties, who remember the Usenet shutdowns and have little appetite for a second.

The second — the insurrection school — holds that the gardens can be disrupted by a sufficiently motivated coalition of small networks, perhaps in partnership with municipal broadband projects or public broadcasters. This view is most popular among engineers in their twenties, who did not experience the Usenet shutdowns and are therefore, their elders say, correspondingly less discouraged.

The third — and this is the view that Okonkwo, and roughly a third of our respondents, expressed — holds that the question itself is wrong. The independent web, they argue, was never about being independent of the commercial networks. It was about being legible to them: reachable, addressable, traceable. The work, on this reading, is not to build an alternative but to remain, stubbornly, a destination that can still be found.

“I do not need you to visit me every day. I need you to be able to find me if you want to.”

— Miriam Okonkwo, archivist

V. An Epilogue, Written in August

On August 14, while this article was in final edits, the Independent Host Collective announced that it had secured a ten-year peering agreement with a regional network in Portugal — the first such agreement, to our knowledge, signed by an independent coalition since 2018. The terms were not disclosed, but the collective’s director, a 34-year-old engineer named Rafaela Santos, told me in a phone call that they were “not unreasonable.”

She paused, and then added, with the careful precision of someone who has spent the last decade learning exactly which words will be remembered: “We do not pretend this is the beginning of anything. It is a door. We will keep it unlocked.”

Notes & Corrections

  1. An earlier version of this article misattributed the 2020 peering figure to Caida. The measurement was performed jointly by Caida and the University of Twente’s RIPE Atlas team.
  2. Miriam Okonkwo’s archive, previously referred to as “The Web Comic Commons,” is officially named “Funnies 1998–2011.”
