Entry 03 — The Photographer

Ida Vestergaard Keeps Records

Eleven years, eighty-seven weddings, one darkroom that smells of fixer. This is the part of the practice that is not a photograph.

Story

I Started Because Nobody Had Photographed My Grandmother’s Wedding

My grandmother married in a church in Nakskov in 1961. There is one photograph of that day, and it shows the car, not the couple. I have thought about that missing frame my whole working life.

I trained as a press photographer at a regional paper in Malmö, where the job was to be in the room and out of the story within ninety seconds. That is still the discipline I bring to a wedding. I spent four years shooting funerals, council meetings, and harvest festivals before anyone asked me to photograph a marriage, and I learned more about how people behave when they think they are not being watched in those four years than in anything since.

The first wedding I shot was in 2014, for friends, for a bottle of wine and the cost of film. I got almost everything wrong: too much flash, too many instructions, and a shutter speed that ruined the first dance. I still print that frame of the bride’s shoes by the door, because it is the one thing I got right and it reminds me what the job actually is.

Lenscraft is a one-person studio by design. I do not subcontract, I do not run a second camera from an assistant, and I do not take more than eighteen weddings a year so that each one gets the editing time it needs. Everything that comes out of this studio has passed through my hands twice: once in the room, and once at the light table.

I work from a studio on Refshalevej with a bath of fixer in the back room, and I develop all my black-and-white negative myself. Colour goes to a lab in Copenhagen that has been scanning my film since 2017 and knows exactly how I like the shadows to sit.

2014 — First wedding

Friends of a friend, forty guests, a rented hall. I shot four rolls, overexposed two, and understood for the first time that a wedding is a documentary assignment where the subjects are also the clients.

2016 — The darkroom on Refshalevej

A former boat workshop with running water and no windows on one wall. Every black-and-white negative from entry 014 onward has been developed by me, in that room, at 20 °C.

2018 — Medium format

Sixteen frames per roll instead of thirty-six. The camera slowed the day down and the pictures got better for it. I have used a 6×4.5 back on every wedding since, paired with a 35 mm body for the dark hours.

2021 — First wedding abroad

Twelve guests, two countries of family, and a rule that nobody could speak Danish at the tables. It taught me to photograph a room I cannot hear, which is most of what travel weddings ask of a person.

2024 — The ten-year archive

Negatives are stored in numbered boxes, scans are held on three media in two locations, and every client gallery is guaranteed online for a decade. The policy exists because I have already seen one photographer’s hard drives fail a wedding.

What I Will and Will Not Do

Eleven years of weddings is enough to form a few firm opinions. These are the ones that have not changed.

If you want to see each other before the ceremony, I will photograph it honestly and from a distance. I will not ask you to walk away and come back again while I check my exposure. The reaction is the picture; everything else is furniture.

Rain, a late registrar, an uncle who cried too hard, an empty chair that mattered. A record that leaves those out is not a record of your day, it is a mood board. If you would rather not see a frame later, tell me and I will pull it from the gallery — but I will have taken it.

Volume is a way of hiding an inability to choose. Every gallery I deliver is edited twice, sequenced in the order the day happened, and signed off by me at the light table. You get fewer pictures and you will actually look at them.

Most photographers leave when the floor clears. The leaving is where the day becomes visible: coats borrowed by strangers, the couple counting the envelopes, someone asleep in a chair they have claimed for years. That is why the coverage in every package runs to the exit.

Eighteen weddings a year is the ceiling, and no Saturday is double-booked. It is also why four dates in 2027 are already on hold while you read this.

“We asked her to be invisible and she took that instruction as a professional standard rather than a compliment. On the day we forgot she was there, which is exactly what you want from the person holding the record.”
Elin & Markus — Bohuslän, 2025
