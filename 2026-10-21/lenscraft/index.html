Elin og Markus — Bohuslän Coast

They married on a granite shelf forty minutes from the nearest town, in wind strong enough to require hairpins borrowed from the groom’s grandmother. We shot the whole afternoon on Portra 400 and never used a flash. The ceremony ran eleven minutes late, which is the only way these things should run.

Method

How a Lenscraft Day Runs

No shot list read aloud, no forced family photographs, and no photographer asking you to hold still. I stay at the edge of the room until the room forgets I am there.

Meet the photographer

I start two hours before the ceremony and photograph whatever is already happening: the dress on the door, the nervous uncle, the list of readings folded into quarters. Nothing is staged, and nothing is repeated for the camera.

A Contax 645 and a Nikon FM2, both carried, both loaded. Film decides the pace of a day: thirty-six frames forces me to wait for the moment instead of shooting through it. Digital backs up the vows and the dark parts of the evening.

Negative sleeves come back from the lab on Thursday and go into a labelled box with your names on it. You get a preview of twenty frames within seven days, which is usually enough to stop you refreshing the gallery page.

Between four hundred and one thousand finished frames, sequenced in the order the day happened, plus the scans of every usable negative. Nothing is cropped to fit an algorithm, and the archive stays online for ten years.

“Ida sent us a photograph of our grandmothers sitting together, and neither family had noticed it happen. That frame is now the only record we have of the two of them in the same room.”
Marianne & Peter Holm — Frederiksborg, 2024
