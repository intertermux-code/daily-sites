The Ninety-Day Battery

Grid-scale storage was supposed to be a manufacturing problem. It has turned out to be an accounting one — a fight over what ninety days means, who gets to define it, and which number a utility is allowed to put in a filing.

Reported by Theo Brandt
Photographs Wren Castellanos
Published 21 October 2026
Reading time 21 minutes
Filed from Columbus, Ohio

In a rented warehouse outside Columbus, Ohio, five megawatts of sodium-ion cells have been quietly winning an argument for nineteen months. The warehouse belongs to a utility that asked not to be named in this story, which is itself the first clue about how the industry feels about what is happening inside it. The cells are not exotic. They were assembled in a plant that made forklift batteries until 2023. The chemistry is old — sodium-ion was demonstrated in the laboratory in 1981 and abandoned within a decade because lithium was better at the thing everyone cared about then, which was fitting energy into a phone.

What the warehouse is demonstrating is a number. Almost every storage company you have heard of will quote you a duration: four hours, eight hours, sometimes twelve. What a grid operator actually needs is not a duration but a season — a way to hold energy for the three weeks in January when the wind stops and the demand curves go vertical. The industry’s shorthand for that is the ninety-day battery, and right now nobody can tell you honestly when one will exist.

“The honest sentence is: we know the physics, we do not know the cost,” Elena Vasquez, who leads storage modelling at the national lab that audited the Kirkwood site, told me. She has spent eleven years watching storage claims age badly. “Every battery is a thermodynamics argument someone lost politely.”

Section 01The Number Nobody Will Sign

Fourteen storage companies publish a degradation curve. Eight of them define cycle life as the point at which a cell falls to eighty percent of nameplate capacity. Four use seventy-five. Two — both of them promising, both of them funded — define it as the point at which the cell can no longer complete its duty cycle, a formulation that lets a battery be “alive” for as long as the operator keeps it online.

That is the whole problem in one paragraph. There is no industry standard for what a dead battery is, so every lifetime claim is a claim about a definition as much as about a material. Utilities, which must file their assumptions with a regulator, are the ones forced to choose. Three of the four grid operators I spoke to said they now write the definition into the contract rather than trusting the datasheet.

“We had a vendor tell us the product lasted fifteen years,” said Amara Whitfield, who runs resource planning for a midwestern cooperative with 340,000 customers. “We said, at what depth of discharge, at what temperature, cycling how often? And the answer turned out to be a spreadsheet we were not allowed to see.”

Section 02Sodium, Sulfur and the Long Shadow of Lithium

The reason sodium came back has nothing to do with energy density and everything to do with a supply chain that stopped making sense in 2022. Sodium is washing-soda ash. It is mined in Wyoming and processed in Ohio. There is no mine in the world whose output a European battery plant depends on, no concentration of refining that could turn a raw material into a diplomatic instrument.

  • Sodium-ion gives up roughly a third of the energy per kilogram that lithium iron phosphate manages, which is irrelevant if the battery lives in a field rather than a car. Its real advantage is cost and the fact that it can be discharged to zero volts for transport without going into a failure mode.
  • Iron-air pushes the same trade much further — round-trip efficiency in the low sixties, which means you lose a third of the energy you put in, but the media is rust and the capital cost is falling faster than anyone forecast.
  • Flow batteries decouple power from energy, so you scale duration by buying more tanks rather than more stacks. This is the only architecture in which ninety days is not a chemistry problem at all.
  • Compressed air and gravity remain, operationally speaking, a joke the sector tells itself at conferences. Two of the eleven pilots announced in 2024 have delivered measurable energy.

Section 03A Fable of Two Pilots

Kirkwood is a success with an asterisk. It has met every availability target set in its contract, and it has done so while costing the cooperative more per megawatt-hour than the peaker plant it was supposed to displace — because the peaker plant gets to externalise the thing nobody wants to price, which is the privilege of being the only option on a hot evening.

Eight thousand kilometres south, a second pilot tells the opposite story. The Atacama installation in northern Chile is a flow battery attached to a solar farm that regularly produces more than the transmission line can carry. Its job is not arbitrage. Its job is to stop curtailment, and curtailment is free money you are setting on fire, so the economics resolve in the first year rather than the tenth.

Atacama’s cells are replaceable individually, in the field, without taking the stack offline — a design decision that matters more to a ninety-day battery than any breakthrough in the literature. The Ohio site’s operators will not discuss swaps at all, which strongly suggests there are some.

Section 04The Supply Chain Nobody Certified

Sodium-ion’s raw material is clean. Its components are not. Hard-carbon anodes — the material that makes sodium cells work at all — come overwhelmingly from three producers in two countries, and the binder chemistry is a closely guarded trade secret at each of them. When I asked two cell makers whether they could name a second qualified source, both changed the subject.

A battery is a chemical plant wearing the costume of an electronic component. We keep pretending we can certify it like a microchip. Dr. Ines Køhler, Technical University of Denmark

There is also the question of what happens to the cells after they fail. Sodium-ion is exempt from the lithium-battery recycling rules that took effect in the European Union in 2023, because the regulators wrote those rules around the metals worth recovering. The pilots now running will reach end of life in the early 2030s with no defined destination. Four of the companies I contacted said they assumed a municipal landfill pathway. One said it had begun designing for recovery and would not say more.

Section 05How to Read the Claim in the Press Release

After eleven years of these announcements, the desk has developed a cheap set of tests. They are not sophisticated. They work.

  • Look for the definition of end of life before you look for the number. If the release does not state a capacity threshold and a cycle depth, the lifetime figure is decorative.
  • Ask whether the units are cells, modules or containers. A record set by a pouch cell in a climate-controlled lab and a record set by a shipping-container system in Arizona are not the same record.
  • Count the months of continuous service rather than the total test hours. Calendar ageing is the mechanism that kills long-duration batteries and the one almost never reported.
  • Check who paid for the demonstration. Site-hosted pilots where the utility took the performance risk tell you four times more than vendor-funded ones, in our sample of twenty-six announcements since 2021.

None of this is a reason to be cynical about the warehouse outside Columbus. Nineteen months at 91.4 percent is genuinely good, and it is better than anything sodium-ion managed three years ago. The point is narrower: the technology is arriving on a schedule, and the language around it is not. Until a duration claim comes with a definition attached, it is marketing — and the utilities know it, which is why they are now writing the definitions themselves, one contract at a time, slowly, in a sector that cannot afford to be slow.

“We are not waiting for a miracle,” Whitfield told me. “We are waiting for somebody to tell us what they mean.”

Notes, Method and Corrections

Everything we rely on is listed here. Corrections are dated and never silently edited.

We collected published datasheets and investor materials from 34 long-duration storage companies between March and September 2026, then requested the underlying definition of end-of-life in writing. Fourteen answered. Six of those provided a document; eight provided a verbal description that we could not verify. Every figure quoted in this feature is taken from a document we hold or from an audit filing, and we list which in the data appendix available on request.

Key Findings

  1. There is no agreed industry definition of battery end of life, so lifetime claims are not comparable across vendors.
  2. Nineteen months of continuous service at 91.4% capacity is the strongest long-duration result we could verify in the United States this year.
  3. The Chilean pilot’s economics work because it is paid to stop curtailment, not to arbitrage price.
  4. Hard-carbon anode supply is concentrated in three producers; no second source is qualified anywhere we checked.
  5. Sodium-ion cells reaching end of life in the 2030s currently have no defined recycling pathway.
