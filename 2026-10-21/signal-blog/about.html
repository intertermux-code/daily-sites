What We Think We Are For

A short statement, because long ones are usually evasions.

Most technology coverage is written for people who already work in technology. We try to write for the rest of the people who are affected by it — the ratepayer, the county commissioner, the nurse whose hospital runs on an unmaintained library, the engineer being told to expect a revolution next quarter. That means explaining the mechanism, not just the announcement.

Principle 01

Machinery Over Momentum

We are less interested in what is trending than in what is load-bearing. A story about a router’s telemetry beats a story about a keynote, every week, unless the keynote changes what a router does.

Principle 02

Numbers With Their Receipts

Every figure we publish is traceable to a document, a dataset or a named source. Where we cannot verify a number, we say so in the sentence, not in a footnote.

Principle 03

Corrections Are the Product

We ran nine corrections last year against 44 published stories. Each one stays visible at the bottom of its piece, dated, with what changed. A publication without a public corrections log is a brochure.

Independence, in practice

We take no money from companies we cover, and we disclose the foundation grants we do take — named, with amounts, on the same page that lists our staff. Reporters may not accept press trips, review units that are not returned, or speak at a paid industry event. When a source gives us something for free, the story says so.

Why a cooperative

Staff own the publication collectively; the accountant holds a veto on anything touching payroll. Decisions about what to publish are made by the editors, and the readers’ elected representative sits on the board with a voice but no vote on editorial matters. It is slower than a founder-led magazine and considerably less prone to one bad week of judgement.

A letter from the editor

We Would Rather Be Late Than Comfy

People ask what the point of a small, slow technology publication is when the internet is full of fast ones. The answer is that speed is not a virtue when you are describing a system. A battery datasheet, a fab permit, a router’s diagnostic payload — these things take a week of reading documents, and the week is the story.

We publish less than we could. Three hundred and sixty-seven stories since 2016, most of them longer than the industry norm, several of them months in reporting. Two of our pieces have been retracted by us; both are listed below with the reasons.

The other thing readers fund is the willingness to say no. We turned down a nine-month sponsorship last spring from a company that would have covered our Berlin desk for a year, because the contract reserved the right to be informed before publication. That is not a moral victory, it is a subscription model working as intended.

If that sounds like the kind of publication you want to keep alive, the fastest way is to take the Thursday Briefing. It costs nothing and it is what pays for the reporting.

— Nadia

How We Work, In Detail

The policies we actually follow. Open one at a time — they are short.

Newsletter policy in the FAQ →

Anonymity requires that the information be unobtainable on the record, that the source be at genuine professional risk, and that an editor — not the reporter alone — approve it. We tell every anonymous source which facts about them must be describable to readers, and we do not grant blanket anonymity for a whole story. Two sources have been named in our archive without consent, and both times the decision was made in writing before publication.

No press trips, no paid speaking, no conference passes supplied by a subject. Review hardware is accepted only when returned within thirty days or donated with the receipt published. Travel for reporting is paid by Signal; if a host pays for a train ticket we say so inside the story. In 2025 we declined 14 invitations and disclosed 3 that we accepted under these conditions.

Quantitative stories ship with the dataset or an explicit account of why we cannot — usually a licensing restriction or a privacy obligation. Notebooks go to subscribers on request and to any researcher who asks in good faith. Charts must survive greyscale printing; if a colour encodes meaning, the label encodes it again.

Corrections are appended, dated and never worded as an apology for the reader’s confusion. We do not silently edit a published story except to remove a legal error or fix a broken link; anything substantive appears below the text. Two pieces have been withdrawn: a 2022 profile of a hydrogen venture that relied on a source we could not corroborate, and a 2024 explainer whose central figure was computed against the wrong denominator. Both remain in the archive with the reason stated.

See a live example in this week’s feature →

Every subject of a critical story gets the specific allegations in writing and 48 hours, extended on request where the material is technical. We publish their response in full if it is short, and quote it substantially if it is not. We do not grant final approval of the text to anyone outside the masthead — a distinction that has ended two sponsorship conversations.

Reporters may use language models to summarise their own transcripts, to check a spelling of a chemical process, and to write throwaway scripts. No generated text is published as prose, no source material is uploaded to a third-party model, and no illustration ships without a named human making it. When we cannot do something by hand, we say so in the story.

Ten Years, Briefly

From a newsletter with 40 readers to a cooperative with eight staff.

2016

A newsletter in a garage in Columbus

Nadia Okonjo and two laid-off engineers start writing about substations. Forty subscribers, a spreadsheet, no name worth keeping.

2018

First story with a document appendix

A report on smart-meter rollout ships with 180 pages of procurement filings. Three utilities request the reader list; we tell them no for the first time.

2019

We go reader-funded and rename the thing Signal

Advertising stops entirely. Revenue drops 61 percent in a quarter, then recovers past the old number within a year at 4,200 paying readers.

2022

The first retraction

A hydrogen profile relies on one uncorroborated source. It stays in the archive with the reason published — a decision that shaped the corrections policy above.

2024

Berlin desk opens

Saoirse Nolan starts instrumenting consumer devices against European disclosure rules. Our open rate is now measured, not estimated.

2026

367 stories in the archive, 38,400 Thursday readers

The long-duration storage series begins. The accountant publishes the annual disclosure on 3 February, as always, before the board reads it.
