Seven programs. One standard: you choose your own food.

Everything below runs year-round unless noted, costs nothing to use, and asks for no proof of income. If one program does not fit your week, another probably does — and the front desk will help you stack them.

Program one

Weekly Grocery Distributions

Seven neighbourhood sites, every week of the year, including the weeks most pantries close. You do not stand in a line and you do not receive a pre-packed bag — you walk an actual shelf layout and pick what your household eats, guided by a volunteer who is holding a clipboard for themselves, not for you.

A typical visit takes eleven minutes and leaves a family of four with twelve to fifteen pounds of food: fresh produce, dairy, bakery, protein, and pantry staples. Take what you need; there is no per-household cap.

  • Seven sites, Tue–Sat

    From 9am in Grove Park to 6pm at Hollis Heights, so a shift worker and a grandparent with a school run can both make it.

  • Bring a bag, or borrow one

    We keep 400 reusable tote bags at each site and never ask you to bring your own. Cart help is available at every location.

  • No ID, no income test

    Sign a one-line sheet with a first name if you like. We have never asked anyone for a pay stub and we never will.

Program two

The Mobile Market Bus

A retired city bus with its seats ripped out and refrigerated shelving bolted in, parked in four blocks that are a two-mile walk from the nearest grocery store. Fresh produce costs a quarter of supermarket price, and it is free with a Bright Harvest card — which takes ninety seconds to get, at the bus, with no paperwork.

The bus carries the produce that arrives too ripe for a distribution box: stone fruit in July, tomatoes in September, the winter squash that nobody wants in March. Neighbours tell us it is the only place in the valley where they buy peaches.

Program three

Gleaning & the Fresh Line

Every season, growers near Grove Park plant more than the market will buy — or the crop sizes wrong, or a buyer cancels a week out. Rather than plough it under, they call us, and a crew of volunteers goes out to harvest it. That is the Fresh Line: produce picked in the morning and on a kitchen counter the same evening.

Last season gleaning put 186,000 pounds of food into distributions — 41% of everything we moved. It is also the reason a box of sweet corn reaches the Hollis Heights site in August instead of a shelf of filler pasta.

Program four

Community Kitchens

Thursdays at the Grove Park middle school cafeteria and the Hollis Heights church hall. A chef and eight neighbours cook a double batch of whatever came off the vans that week, eat together, and everyone leaves with a labelled container for their own freezer. Childcare is in the next room; interpreters for Spanish, Vietnamese, and ASL are booked on request.

People come for the food and stay for the table. Three of our current volunteer leads first walked in for a Thursday kitchen because they wanted somewhere to be at seven o’clock.

Program five

Home-Delivered Senior Boxes

Nine routes, Wednesday mornings, for neighbours over sixty-five who cannot drive to a site — including everyone at the Cedar River assisted living block and the 210 households on the Hollis elevator list. A driver carries the box to the door, and stays for a minute if that is what is available.

Boxes are built for one or two people: no bulk tins you cannot finish, nothing that needs a stove you no longer use, and always something green. Our drivers log 1,400 doorsteps a month, which makes them the closest thing we have to a welfare check.

Program six

Baby & Toddler Groceries

Infant formula, shelf-stable milk, iron-fortified cereal, jarred food, and diapers — the four items our partners almost never donate and the four that break a household budget fastest. Available at every distribution point without a separate visit, plus a standing crate at Hollis Community Clinic for prenatal appointments.

We also stock a lending shelf of breast pumps, washable inserts, and size-three car seats, because a neighbour asked for one in 2019 and we have never found a good reason to say no.

Program seven

Nutrition Classes & Benefits Navigation

Free six-week cooking classes built around what actually arrives at the depot, taught by two of our own neighbours who completed the program last year. The navigation desk next door helps with SNAP and WIC applications, utility arrears, and the school meal forms — an average of 340 appointments a month.

Class graduates get first refusal on the gleaning crews, which pay a small stipend, and a permanent seat at the Thursday kitchen table.

The practical part

Eligibility, in plain language.

Do I have to prove that I am short on money?

No. Bright Harvest uses the federal emergency food threshold, which means anyone who says they need food needs food. We do not ask for pay stubs, benefit letters, immigration status, or an explanation. If you are reading this on your phone at 4pm deciding whether you can make dinner, you qualify.

How often can I come?

Once a week per household, at any site — you do not have to return to the same one. If a week is not enough, tell the front desk. Roughly 1,900 households also use the Mobile Market or a senior box, and combining programs is normal, not a favour we are extending.

What if I cannot carry the box to a car?

Every site has a cart and at least one volunteer whose whole job is walking food to a vehicle. Ask for a “runner” at the door. For neighbours who cannot get to a site at all, the senior box routes deliver to the door on Wednesdays regardless of age — the criterion is mobility, not a birthday.

Can someone collect for me?

Yes. A friend, neighbour, or family member can pick up your household box with a first name and a rough address. No signed authorisation, no ID check. If you want to be sure they do not double up with a box you already collected, text the depot and we will note it on the sheet.

Do you accommodate allergies, diabetes, and baby food?

Peanut-free, low-sodium, and gluten-free staples are stocked at every site, and the shelf cards list allergens. Infant formula, cereal, jarred food, and diapers are available without a separate visit through the Baby & Toddler program. Tell us once and it goes on your household sheet.

Is the food safe?

Every item is checked against a date and quality standard before it is shelved, cold-chain temperatures are logged hourly, and our depot is inspected twice a year by the county. About 6% of what we collect is diverted to compost because it did not pass — we would rather explain a gap than hand over something we would not eat.

Behind the counter

The unglamorous parts that make fresh food possible.

Year-round

The Orchard Lane Garden

Ninety raised beds on land the city lent us in 2018, plus a greenhouse that runs all winter. Half the harvest goes to distributions; the other half goes to whoever is learning to grow in the Saturday class.

3,100 pounds in 2025

Winter

Seed & Seedling Library

Free seed packets and April tomato starts, no card required. It exists because a neighbour asked in 2021 whether she could grow her own cilantro, and now 640 households plant something every spring.

Ask at the front desk

Partnership

The Grower Agreement

Twenty-one farms sign a standing agreement to call us before they call a composter. We pay nothing and ask for nothing except a harvest date, and they get a same-day pickup guarantee.

41 new partners this year

Training

Food Safety Certification

A free, four-hour county-accredited course for volunteers and partner grocers, taught twice a month at the depot. It is the reason our rejection rate at county inspection has been zero since 2016.
