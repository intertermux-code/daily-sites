Told by the people who lived them, published how they approved it.

Every story here was read back to the person it is about, and changed where they asked. Some use a first name only. None of them were written to make you feel worse about your dinner than you already do.

Marisol Reyes · Grove Park

“I stopped skipping lunch to save bus fare.”

Marisol started a night-cleaning crew at the Riverbend hotel in the spring of 2024, the month her hours were cut from thirty-eight to twenty-two. She was thirty-three, raising a nine-year-old and a four-year-old, and paying $780 a month for a two-bedroom above a laundromat on Alder Street.

“The worst part was not being hungry. It was that my daughter started noticing which foods I would not buy, and then she stopped asking for them. That is a child doing arithmetic no child should be doing.”

A neighbour mentioned the Tuesday distribution in passing, and Marisol went because she wanted milk. She walked out with eleven pounds of food and a card for the Thursday kitchen, where she has now cooked every week since February.

She works the kitchen as a host once a month — the shift where she sets tables and runs the childcare room — and she is finishing the six-week nutrition class in November. Her hours at the hotel are back to thirty-four. The arithmetic stopped.

What changed, in her words

  • Nobody asked her a single question about money

    “I had already filled out forms at three places. Here I said my name and they handed me a bag for the shelves.”

  • The kitchen solved the freezer problem

    Cooking double batches meant she stopped throwing away leftovers that no one wanted reheated on a Wednesday.

  • Childcare made it possible at all

    “If I had to pay a sitter to sit in a class about beans, I would not have come. They just had a room with a woman who knows what she is doing.”

Partner grower · Delacroix Family Farm

“Forty years of growing. I never threw a good apple away until I had to.”

Auguste Delacroix farms ninety acres eleven miles east of the depot. In 2018 a buyer cancelled twelve tons of second-grade apples nine days before harvest, and he drove them to a landfill because no one in the county would take them.

Two weeks later a Bright Harvest van appeared at his gate with a scale, a pallet jack, and a one-page agreement: call us before you plough, and we will be there the same afternoon. He has called every season since.

“What I did not expect was the standard. They will not take my worst box. They stand there and sort it with me and say, no, Auguste, these people deserve better than that. That is the nicest thing anyone has ever done for my apples.”

Last season the Delacroix farm sent 41,000 pounds — kale in February, apples through June, tomatoes in September. It funded roughly 34,000 meals and did not cost Bright Harvest a dollar of purchase money, only diesel and a driver’s hour.

From the staff side

Renée on why we do not photograph anyone’s face at a distribution.

Renée Alvarado has run the depot floor for nine years, and she is the reason the photography policy on this page exists. She has turned down two magazine commissions and a regional Emmy package because neither would agree to publish without identifiable neighbours at the shelves.

The policy, in full

  • Consent is specific and revocable

    A neighbour approves the exact photo, the exact page, and the name they want used. Withdraw it and we take the image down within one working day, everywhere, including our own social archives.

  • No one is shot mid-transaction

    Distribution floors are off-limits to cameras during open hours. Every image of a household box on this site was staged afterwards, with a volunteer standing in.

  • Stories pay, when they can

    Anyone who sits for a long-form interview chooses a $75 grocery credit or a donated box in their name. It is not a fee for a story; it is the same thing we hand a volunteer at the end of a shift.
