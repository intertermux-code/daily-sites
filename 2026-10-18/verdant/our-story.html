It Began With a Spreadsheet

In 2019 our founder listed every ingredient she could find on the bottles in her own bathroom. There were four hundred and six of them, and she could not explain why a single one was there.

01

Origin

Forty Bottles, No Answers

Ida Verdant ran a small pharmacy counter in Nørrebro and spent her afternoons explaining the same thing to the same customers: that a higher price had nothing to do with a better formula. The shelf was full of products with thirty ingredients where six did the work and twenty-four existed to make the bottle smell like a spa.

She wrote the spreadsheet, then wrote a second one with the ingredients she could defend. Nine lines. That list became the brief for the first three formulas, and it is still the rule the lab works to: if you cannot say why it is in there, it is not.

Seven years later we make eleven products and we have turned down forty-two ideas that would have been number twelve.

02

The valley

Eleven Days on Cedar Racks

Most botanical extracts are dried in a kiln in four hours. It is cheaper, it is faster, and it destroys the polysaccharides that make fern extract calming in the first place.

In 2021 we found a valley north of Kyoto where a family of four still cuts bracken by hand in August and lays it on cedar racks in an open barn. It takes eleven days. The leaf comes out green rather than olive, and the water it gives up smells like a forest floor after rain.

We buy that whole harvest every year at roughly forty per cent above the regional rate, which is the only honest way to insist that nobody rushes the racks. When the harvest is short — it was in 2023 — we pause the Elixir rather than substitute a supplier.

03

Ingredient index

Six Things We Put on Skin, and Why

Tap any name below to see the formulas that carry it. Concentrations are the ones we can defend with our own panel data.

3.1

Niacinamide — Vitamin B3

Why: it reduces the look of uneven tone and supports the barrier, and it does that at five per cent. We do not push to ten, because our panel reported more flushing above six and there is no evidence the extra works harder. Where: Clarifying Serum, Morning Dew.

3.2

Fern & Bracken Extract

Why: eleven-day air drying preserves water-binding polysaccharides that calm visible redness within about twenty minutes of application. Where: Fern Recovery Elixir, Botanist Hand Balm, Aromatic Body Oil.

3.3

Peppermint Hydrosol

Why: the water left after steam distillation, which most brands discard. It replaces plain water as the first ingredient in our cleanser, so the base itself does something. Where: Leaf & Stem Cleansing Gel, Leaf Light Face Fluid.

3.4

Bisabolol from Evening Primrose

Why: it is the fraction of chamomile that actually soothes, isolated so we never need fragrance to make a product feel pleasant. Where: Calm Repair Oil, Sun Veil Fluid.

3.5

Three-Weight Hyaluronic Acid

Why: one molecular size sits on the surface, one holds water in the upper layers, one is small enough to travel. A single weight gives you a tacky film and nothing underneath. Where: Morning Dew Moisturiser, Leaf Light Face Fluid.

3.6

Camellia & Olive Squalane

Why: both are stable, both mimic the lipids skin already makes, and neither comes from sharks. We press camellia seed in small batches every six weeks so it never sits in a warm drum turning rancid. Where: Night Bloom Facial Oil, Aromatic Body Oil.

04

The bench

Two Hundred and Forty Volunteers

Every formula goes through a six-week panel of 240 people across four skin tones and three climate zones before it can be sold. We test in Copenhagen winter, Singapore humidity, and a dry continental season, because a moisturiser that behaves in one of those is not automatically honest about the others.

Volunteers keep a daily log and are paid in money, not product, so nobody feels obliged to be kind. We publish the drop-out rate alongside the results — the Enzyme Polish lost eleven per cent of its panel in week two and we rebuilt it twice.

Nothing is tested on animals, at any stage, by us or by any supplier, in any market that would require it. That is why we do not sell in certain countries, and we would rather stay that way.

05

Timeline

Seven Years, Eleven Bottles

  1. 2019

    The Spreadsheet

    Ida writes nine defensible ingredients on one page and asks a contract lab in Aarhus to build a cleanser around them.

  2. 2020

    Three Formulas, One Market Stall

    Cleansing Gel, Morning Dew and the first Niacinamide Serum sell out at a Saturday market in Nørrebro in four hours.

  3. 2021

    The Valley Contract

    Two weeks in Kyoto produce a five-year agreement for the whole wild bracken harvest, at forty per cent above the regional rate.

  4. 2022

    Front-Label Law of Our Own Making

    We move the full ingredient deck onto the front of the carton and lose two stockists who say it “looks like a medicine.”

  5. 2023

    The Short Harvest

    Fern supply falls thirty per cent. We pause the Elixir for nineteen weeks rather than change suppliers, and tell everyone why on the product page.

  6. 2024

    Refills Begin

    Glass bottles are redesigned so the pump and cap survive three refills. Return two empties, receive three pouches.

  7. 2026

    Eleven, and Still Eleven

    Sun Veil and Aromatic Body Oil join the range. Number twelve is on a shelf in the workshop, unfinished, waiting for a reason.

06

Refills

Keep the Glass, Send the Empties

A glass bottle is heavy to ship once and absurd to throw away. Our refills arrive as lightweight pouches holding the same formula, and you pour them into the bottle you already like the weight of.

Post two clean empties back in the prepaid sack and three refills cost a third less than three new bottles. The pump and cap are rated for at least three refills; after that, drop the whole thing at any of our six Copenhagen points and we will separate it.

In 2025 refills covered forty-one per cent of what we shipped. The number we want is closer to eighty, and we would get there faster if the pouch itself were compostable rather than recyclable.
