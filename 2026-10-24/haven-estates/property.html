The House

Ninety Minutes of Walking, Then the Numbers

Ashcombe was built for Edmund Hale, who bound books for a living and insisted the studio face north so the colour of an inlay would read the same at four o’clock as at ten. The builder was a Hampstead man called Wigg, and between them they produced a house that has never been much altered and never much advertised.

You enter through a timber porch into a hall with a tiled floor and a staircase that turns twice. The drawing room keeps its box shutters, its coal grates, and a bay wide enough to seat six. What is new is deliberately quiet: a 2019 rear return in lime plaster and oak, with a single rooflight that frames one copper beech and nothing else.

Above, the third floor was the family’s attic and is now the principal bedroom, dressing room and bathroom, with a view across the heath that does not get blocked by anybody’s planning application. Two further floors hold five bedrooms, a study bedroom, and the studio, which still smells faintly of turpentine.

The garden is walled on three sides, half lawn, half kitchen beds, with a greenhouse that has been standing since 1954 and still works. Parking is on gravel for three behind remote gates. There is no service charge, because there is nobody to charge it.
