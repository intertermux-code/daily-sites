Chapter I — The Argument

Twenty-Two Ingredients, Nine Of Them Decoration

Ana Varela spent eight years formulating for other people’s brands in the Portuguese contract-lab belt. The brief was always the same: a working base, then a shelf of “hero” additions the marketing team had seen elsewhere. Each one earned a place on the label and cost the stability of the formula.

In 2019 she took a notebook to a small Bonfim unit with a rent she could survive for eighteen months, and wrote a rule she still keeps on the wall above the homogeniser: an ingredient stays only if removing it changes how the skin behaves at twenty-eight days. Thirty-one candidates failed that test in the first year.

“Fragrance is not an ingredient. It is a reason people stop using a good formula.”

Dr. Ana VarelaFounder & Formulator
Chapter II — The Wall

Learning From The Stratum Corneum

Skin does not want ingredients; it wants its own lipids back. The corneocyte layer is roughly a 50:25:15:10 balance of ceramides, cholesterol, free fatty acids and esters, and every irritation we had been formulating around was a disruption of that ratio.

Barrier Serum 3.4 is our translation of that ratio into something that can live in a pump at room temperature. Getting ceramide NP, AP and phytosphingosine to disperse without clouding took eleven prototypes; the twelfth was the first batch we sent to testers.

Chapter III — Sourcing

Four Raw Materials, Four Named Suppliers

We buy small enough to visit. Ceramide complex comes from a fermentation house in Gelndorf-adjacent northern Germany that we audit each February; bakuchiol from a babchi grower collective near Indore; squalane from the olive-pomace refinery in Aveiro that supplies half the region’s edible oil; shea from a women’s cooperative in Tamale that grades by hand before it ever reaches a ship.

Water is the exception, and we treat it as one. Porto mains run hard at 19 °dH, so every aqueous phase is double-reverse-osmosed on site and re-tested for conductivity before it touches a batch tank.

Chapter IV — How A Batch Is Made

Cold-Fill, 2,400 Units, Signed By Hand

We heat the aqueous and oil phases separately, combine under vacuum, then cool to 32 °C before filling. Heat is the cheapest way to ruin a lipid, and the most common reason a “barrier” serum stops feeling like one by month three.

  • Every tank is sampled for pH, viscosity and conductivity before release
  • Retain from each batch sits in a 40 °C stability cabinet for six months
  • Cartons carry the batch code, blend date, and the technician who filled it
  • Anything drifting more than 0.2 pH from spec is destroyed, not discounted

What We Deleted

09 — The Short List Of No

Each of these appeared in an early prototype and left because a panel of testers told us their skin cared about it.

Fragrance & Essential Oils

Twelve allergens, one of the most common causes of contact dermatitis, and no effect on skin physiology. Removed from all formulas in 2020.

Botanical Cocktail “Complexes”

Seven extracts dusted at 0.01% to earn a claim. Each one either became a stated percentage or went entirely.

Denatured Alcohol & Menthol

A cool that reads as efficacy and a solvent that undoes the barrier we are rebuilding. Gone after batch 006.

Dry Shampoo Texturisers

Silica-heavy powders that made our cream feel weightless in the first week and matte by the fourth. Replaced with squalane.
