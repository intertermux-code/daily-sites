01

The House and the Flame

We opened with a brick oven, four borrowed chairs, and a stubborn idea: a kitchen should smell like the wood it burns.

Ten winters in, the idea still holds. Every plate that leaves our pass has crossed live coals — including the bread, the desserts, and the lemons we char for the vinaigrette. There is no gas line in this building and no pilot light to fall back on. When the fire goes out, service ends.

Our wood arrives split and stacked from an orchard two hours south: pruned oak and vine, air-dried eighteen months, burned down to a bed of coals we rake to temperature through the evening. It is slow, it is temperamental, and it is the only reason the food tastes the way it does.

03

The Fire Ritual

Service begins at four in the afternoon — two hours before you are allowed through the door.

  1. 01 2:00 PM — The load. Two cases of oak and a armful of vine kindling go in cold. Nothing else happens until the smoke turns from white to blue.
  2. 02 4:15 PM — The collapse. The flame column falls and we rake the coals into three beds: searing, roasting, and holding. Every dish on the menu is assigned to one of them.
  3. 03 6:30 PM — The feed. A cook works the hearth continuously with a long-handled peel, adding splits the width of a wrist. The room temperature rises by four degrees and stays there.
  4. 04 11:40 PM — The rake-out. What is left gets banked with ash and lit tomorrow the same way. We have never used a match twice on the same fire.
“You can hide a bad ingredient in a saucepan. You cannot hide one in front of an open fire.” Nora Bexley — Executive Chef
