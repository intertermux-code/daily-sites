Eleven Years on the Bica Hill

It Started with a Failed Loaf.

In 2014, Ana Ferreira rented a shuttered haberdashery, kept the sewing-machine hook on the wall, and burned four hundred loaves before the fifth hundred sold. This is the short version of a long fermentation.

The Long Proof

How the Shop Grew, Year by Year

2014

A Haberdashery Becomes a Bakery

Ana signs a five-year lease on 42 Rua da Bica do Duarte Belo. The first oven is secondhand, the first starter comes from a bakery in Coimbra by bus, and the first open day ends with nine loaves sold and a broken door handle.

2016

The Stone Mill Arrives by the Window

After two years of buying flour, we start grinding our own. A small granite mill takes the corner seat, and every loaf from then on carries a grain name you could drive to.

2018

Natas, and the Sunday Queue

Miguel Sousa joins from a Belém house and rebuilds our natas from the lamination up. Sundays get a queue; the queue gets a nickname (“the solstice line”); the solstice line gets its own weather forecast joke.

2021

Through the Hard Year

Windows-only sales, delivery by bicycle, and a bread ledger for the neighborhood — take a loaf now, pay when it suits. Not one regular left the tab unpaid. That ledger hangs framed by the till.

2023

Grain Partners, Not Suppliers

We sign direct agreements with four farms across the Alentejo and Beira Baixa, paying a fixed premium price for heritage roquilha and Barbela wheat, whatever the market does.

Today

Eleven Years, Same Corner

Fourteen people, two ovens, one mill, six days a week. The sewing-machine hook is still on the wall, and the fifth-hundred-and-first loaf is still the one we chase.

Why Here

A Hill, a Tram, and Two Marble Tables

The Bica is not a convenient place to run a bakery. Deliveries reverse, the tram rings its bell at closing time, and every sack of flour climbs what tourists call “the famous slope.” We would not trade it.

A bakery is a street organ. It opens at dawn so the street can wake up warm; it sells out so the afternoon stays honest; it closes Mondays so the people inside it stay sane. The neighborhood did not find us — we agreed to be useful, and it showed up.

“We measure the year in regulars: babies who now order their own galão.”

What We Hold To

Four Rules, Written in Flour

Grain with an Address

Every sack is traceable to a farm we have eaten at. If we cannot name the field, we do not mill it.

Time Is an Ingredient

Twelve to thirty-six hours of cold fermentation. No improvers, no shortcuts, no “quick” sourdough.

Sell Out, Honestly

We bake to demand, not to ego. Empty shelves at four are a better result than discounted bread at eight.

Nothing Good Is Wasted

Crusts and yesterday’s buns go to the Graça soup kitchen nightly; scraps go to a pig farm in Arruda.

The People at 5 a.m.

Fourteen Hands, Three of Them Famous

You will meet most of them before your nata cools. Here are the three the regulars write songs about.

Ana Ferreira

Founder & head baker. Runs the starter she named “Avó.” Refuses to sell broa before it sings when tapped.

Miguel Sousa

Pâtissier, laminator of natas, ex-Belém. Claims the perfect pastel has exactly eleven blisters. Bring counter-evidence.

Teresa Alves

Front of house since week two. Keeps the bread ledger, knows ninety-one orders by face, and forgives the 7:29 rush.
