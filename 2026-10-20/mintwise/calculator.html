    The donut is drawn to scale against whichever is larger — your income or your expenses — so an overspend shows as a full ring plus a shortfall line instead of a broken chart. Interest on savings, employer 401(k) matches, and tax refunds are not modelled here; Mintwise adds those once it can read the real balances.

    Reading the number

    What Each Band Actually Buys You

    At this stage the goal is not wealth, it is stopping the borrow. One month of expenses sitting in a separate account changes every decision you make afterwards, because a surprise becomes an inconvenience instead of a debt.

    • Pick one fixed number — often $25 a week — and make it an autopay the day after payday.
    • Do not optimise investments yet. A $500 buffer is worth more than a $500 index position you cannot touch for three days.
    • Expect the first two months to feel tight. That feeling is the cost of the floor, and it stops being news by month three.

    This is where a household stops trading one problem for another. Every extra percentage point here is cheaper to find than the last one, because it comes from defaults rather than willpower.

    • Raising 10% to 15% on $6,000 a month is $300 — roughly one subscription tier, one cheaper insurance quote, and one cancelled gym.
    • Match the money you are already saving before you chase a higher rate. An employer match is a 50–100% return with no behaviour change.
    • Track the rate, not the balance. The balance moves with life; the rate is the habit.

    Above 15% you can start making choices that were previously unavailable: a lower-paying job that suits you better, a deposit, a sabbatical, or leaving a situation on your own timetable rather than the next paycheck’s.

    • Separate money by time horizon — under two years stays in cash or a money-market account, no matter how good the alternative looks.
    • Watch for the creep that follows a raise. If the rate holds flat while income climbs, the saved amount is still growing.
    • Name every goal with a date. “Someday” is not a plan and never survives a slow month.

    Rates this high are usually the product of a specific decision — no car payment, a low-rent market, dual incomes with one career pause — rather than a spending technique. That is worth naming, because it tells you whether the number is durable.

    • Test durability: what happens to the rate in a month with a dental bill and a wedding?
    • Automate the surplus into a place with friction on withdrawal, not a checking account you pass daily.
    • Resist the urge to benchmark against strangers. A sustainable 22% beats a heroic 38% that ends in a rebound month.

    If the shortfall is real, it is usually carried by one of three things: a fixed cost that rose faster than pay, debt payments absorbing both, or a period of genuinely low income that no budget fixes. The first two are negotiable, the third is a timing problem.

    • Re-check housing. A rate above 35% of take-home pay there is the most common single cause of a negative panel.
    • Move every debt minimum to the day after payday so nothing defaults while you reorganise.
    • If income is the problem, the calculator cannot help you — that is a different conversation, and our support team will point you to free counselling rather than a product.
