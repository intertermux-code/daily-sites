Since 2009 · Clerkenwell

We took a ribbon warehouse and filled it with arguments.

Seventeen years, six booksellers, one bindery press, and a rule that has not changed since the first fortnight: nothing gets stocked because somebody in a showroom told us to.

The Beginning

Four shelves, one dodgy radiator.

Marguerite left a publishing job in 2008 with a redundancy cheque, a small loan, and a conviction that Clerkenwell did not need another café with books in it. The unit on Bellman’s Row was cheap because it smelled of machine oil and had no proper lighting. She bought the oil smell.

The first stock was secondhand: four hundred titles from a closing shop in Islington, shelved by eye because there was no time to catalogue. Eleven years on, we still shelve by eye, and we have stopped pretending that is a temporary measure.

Elias came in for a Saturday shift in 2010 and never went back to the job he was meant to be between. Tom arrived with a hand press in the boot of a car. The rest of it is on the timeline below, including the year we were nearly wrong about everything.

Seventeen Years

The shop, built in seven moves.

  1. 2009

    The lease, and the oil smell

    Four hundred secondhand titles, one radiator that only worked on Thursdays, and an opening night where eleven people came and nine of them knew Marguerite.

  2. 2011

    We dig out the cellar

    Six weeks of rubble and a surprise brick vault. The Poetry Cellar opens with a reading by two friends and a borrowed PA that hummed all night. It still hums.

  3. 2013

    The wall of pages

    An estate lot in Walthamstow: eleven crates of an encyclopedia, taken apart for its plates. We mounted the open pages on the cellar wall, where they remain.

  4. 2015

    Tom and the hand press

    A 1950s Albion press arrives in the back yard, and with it the bindery. Repairs first, then editions, then the Saturday benches nobody can stop booking.

  5. 2017

    The long table goes upstairs

    Twenty-two seats around a single Sussex oak slab, funded by six hundred people buying ten-pound shares. The club rota began the month after.

  6. 2021

    Fourteen weeks closed

    We delivered nine hundred and forty parcels by bicycle, kept the phone lines open, and lost two landlords’ worth of hope. The shop did not close, which was mostly down to people turning up to help.

  7. 2024

    An imprint, quietly

    Chapter & Verse Editions: three pamphlets a year, printed in the yard, sold only over this counter. The fourth is out in February.

The Counter

Six people, and their shelves.

Ask any of them what they are reading. It will not be the book you expect, and they will happily talk at length about why.

What We Hold To

Three rules, pinned behind the till.

I

Read It or Do Not Sell It

A shelf talk has to come from someone in this building. It is why the cards are sometimes sharp, occasionally wrong, and never copied from a jacket.

II

The Room Beats the Stock

We would rather clear forty chairs for a Thursday reading than shift four hundred paperbacks. Space is the thing a shop can give that a warehouse cannot.

III

Price the Book, Not the Reader

Ten per cent off for members, no charge for clubs, and a shelf of ninepenny books by the door for whoever is short this week. Nobody is asked why.

Asked at the Counter

Things people genuinely ask us.

Thursday morning, six people, one argument. Anything that gets there has been read by at least one of us, and it stays for a fortnight unless it sells out, which is the only metric we track. Nothing arrives because a sales rep was pleasant about it.

Because if we do not read during the week we cannot honestly recommend on Saturday. Tuesdays are for the stock, the new books nobody has opened yet, and the radiator, which we finally replaced in 2019.

Yes — launches, recordings, quartets, one wedding, and a school group every February. Up to ninety standing, forty seated, Sundays and Monday evenings. The rates and the awkward questions are all on the events page.

There were. Four decades of ribbon warehouse accounts, plus a tin of unbound proofs nobody came back for. Most went to the Museum of the Home in 2018; the proofs are still here, three of them unidentified, and we view them publicly once a year. The next viewing is 7 November.

Finding Us

Four minutes from Farringdon, past the laundrette.

Bellman’s Row runs off Clerkenwell Road, between the laundrette and the place that used to sell scooters. The shopfront is black with gold lettering that a member of staff hand-painted in 2012 and refuses to have redone.
