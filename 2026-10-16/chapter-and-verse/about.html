Section E · The Shop Itself

A rope works, a restaurant, and then the books

Chapter & Verse has occupied 14 Kiln Yard since November 1987. Four of us run it, eleven staff work the desk rota, and the building has not had a wall knocked through since the gallery shelving went up in 1994. This is how it happened, written plainly, with the bad years left in.

Entry E.1

Why a shop like this exists on a street like this one

Kiln Yard is named for the brick kilns that closed in 1911. We are not the first trade to stay.

Adeline Ferris opened the shop in 1987 with two and a half thousand second-hand titles, a borrowed till, and a lease she negotiated on the strength of being the only person who had asked. She had run the booksellers’ counter at the co-operative on Market Street for eleven years and knew exactly which customers walked past a new door without entering. The first month took £340. The second took £612, because somebody reviewed it in the Ashborne Gazette and got the name of the shop wrong in a way that made people curious.

The rule she wrote on the wall by the stair — still there, painted over six times, never removed — was that we describe what we sell. Not “lovely condition”, not “general wear”, but the truth: which hinge, which foxing, which printing, what it is worth and what somebody else would pay. Customers forgave the damp in January and the fact that we were shut on Mondays. They did not forgive being treated as though they could not look at a spine.

In 1991 the shop nearly ended. A chain opened four hundred yards up the street with parking, and by the spring of that year the takings had fallen by two fifths. Adeline put a for-sale notice in the window on a Tuesday and took it down on a Thursday, having spent the intervening two days being told, by people who had never spoken to her, that the shop was the reason they moved to Ashborne. Hal Brennan arrived in 1998 as a Saturday casual at nineteen, bought the business in 2011 when Adeline retired to the coast, and has never once described it as a rescue.

What has changed is the shape of the trade: fewer new hardbacks, many more readers who want the edition explained, and a bindery bench that now keeps two apprentices. What has not changed is the number of things we say no to. No coffee machine, because the smell goes into the paper. No bestseller table at the front, because the bestsellers are in every other shop in the county. No event that requires us to charge a reader more than twelve pounds, because that is the price at which a shop starts telling people who they are.

“Nobody in this town ever needed another shop. They needed a room where somebody had read the thing they were thinking about and would say so out loud.”

Adeline Ferris, founder, from her retirement letter, 2011

Entry E.3

Who you will meet at the desk

Four of us run it; eleven more work the rota. Ask any of us what we are reading.

Hal Brennan

Owner since 2011 · Gothic & the rare case

Came in as a Saturday casual in 1998 at nineteen and has never worked anywhere else. Runs Night Fire, does the buying for the Castle, and reads a Brontë novel once a winter for pleasure rather than business, which he insists is a different activity.

Putting on a shelf: the 1857 Currer Bell issue of Jane Eyre — not the one we have, the one at a fair in Hertford, which we are trying to afford.

Marisol Vega

Buyer, poetry & translation · the Cellar

Arrived in 2014 from a small press in Zaragoza and rebuilt the translation shelf around what the translators actually said. Runs Verse Thursday, the Translation Lab, and the argument about line nineteen, which has now been going for four years.

Putting on a shelf: any of the Andorra small-press series, and a literal crib of Ibsen she made on her kitchen table for the Lab.

Denby Achebe

Events & the children’s shelf · the trestle

Kept the register on paper for four years before we let her put it online, and still writes the wall list by hand. Runs Children’s Hour, the Sunday table reads and the carols, and knows every regular’s children by their first names.

Putting on a shelf: a 1953 Gobbets reprint, and any book whose spine will survive being dropped eleven times.

Also on the desk, and not in the photographs

Ruth Okonjo does non-fiction, philosophy and the essayists, keeps forty-one two-page papers from The Long Table in a box under the till, and runs Middlemarch in Ten Weeks with the patience of a foreman.

Fenna de Vries is the binder in the cellar on Wednesdays, with two apprentices and a standing offer: bring us a paperback you love and we will reback it for five pounds rather than let you throw it out.

Robin Martin buys the rare case, dates everything that comes to a valuation evening, and is the person who will tell you plainly that your inheritance is worth forty pounds and not four hundred.

How the rota works

Nobody here was hired for a temperament; everybody was hired because they had already recommended a book to a stranger and been right. Two evenings a week someone from the shop reads on the register rota, and the kettle is on from quarter to nine. If you ask a question and we do not know the answer, we will find out and write to you — that is not a courtesy, it is how the shop has survived four landlords and one flood.

Access, plainly

The front shop and the Long Room are level with the pavement, with a 760mm clear opening and no step. The Cellar Room is reached by eleven steep stone steps with one handrail and no lift; we hold a chair and a lamp upstairs for anyone who cannot manage them, and every reading in the cellar has one. Both rooms have hearing loops — ask at the desk for the panel code. Large-print editions are ordered like any other book and are not marked on the shelf. Assistance dogs are always welcome; the kettle is on for their people too.

Quiet hours

Tuesday and Wednesday mornings, ten till twelve, are kept quiet: no circles, no trestle, no music, and nobody asked whether they have found what they wanted. It began in 2022 when a reader asked us to, and it has stayed because eleven people now come for it.
