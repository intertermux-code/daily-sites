Appx. A Chronology

Seven years, filed in the order they happened.

No pivot we cannot explain, and no year where we changed the story to suit a funding round.

2019Entry 001

A spreadsheet incident

Dana Okonjo and Tobias Vreck were contract analysts at a freight company. Their reconciliation of the disputed quarter turned into a written register of every definition the business used, with owners named beside each one. People stopped arguing in meetings, and the register was the reason.

2020Entry 002

The register becomes a tool

The pair rebuilt the spreadsheet as a small service that read the warehouse directly and refused to render any figure without a registered definition. Three friends at other companies asked for copies. Pulseboard Systems was incorporated in April, in a rented basement in Portland.

2021Entry 003

First forty workspaces

We turned down a $2.4M seed offer that required us to add per-reader pricing, because charging for readers is how dashboards get hoarded. Two months later we raised $1.9M on our own terms. The 40th customer was a hospital group that needed one definition of “occupied bed”.

2022Entry 004

The watch layer ships

Governance told you what a number meant; it did not tell you when the number moved. Seasonality-aware alerting went out in September, and the average workspace silenced 60% of its existing monitoring noise in the first fortnight.

2023Entry 005

Query engine rewrite

We replaced our own execution path with compile-to-warehouse planning so no data leaves your platform. Median p95 fell from 2.1 seconds to 412 milliseconds, and SOC 2 Type II came through the same quarter.

2024Entry 006

1,000 governed workspaces

Embedded boards arrived so customers could show clients the same registry their own analysts use. Series A of $26M closed in November with one condition written into the term sheet: reader seats stay free, permanently.

2026Entry 007

Eighty-four people, three regions

Portland, Dublin and Singapore. 18,400 metrics under version control across our customer base, and a standing internal rule that no engineer may ship a chart whose definition they cannot cite from the registry.

Appx. B Principles

Five rules we have actually turned down money over.

P-01

A number without an owner is not a number

Every metric in a Pulseboard registry carries a named person accountable for it. We will not build a feature that lets a figure circulate anonymously, because that is how the argument starts again.

P-02

Readers are free, forever

Charging per viewer turns information into a rationed good. Teams hoard boards, screenshots replace dashboards, and the registry rots. It is the single most damaging pricing decision in this category and we will not take it.

P-03

Your warehouse is the source of truth, not us

We compile to your platform and run there. Nothing is copied into our storage to make our caching problem easier. It makes our engineering harder and your security review shorter, which is the right trade.

P-04

Silence beats false precision

If a metric is too unstable to report honestly, the board says so and shows why. We have removed more charts at customer request than we have added, and the trust that replaced them is worth the difference.

P-05

Leaving must be easy

The registry exports as plain SQL and YAML at any time, free, with no support ticket required. A definition you wrote should never be hostage to a vendor, including us.
