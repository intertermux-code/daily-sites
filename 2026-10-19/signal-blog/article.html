The Last Rack in the Building

Nineteen years of in-house hardware ends on a Tuesday in Newark. What replaces it is not the cloud the industry promised — it is somebody else’s cloud, on a contract nobody in the room was allowed to read.

By Devan Rao ◆ Photographs by Anke Fuldner ◆ 14 min read ◆ Published Friday, 16 October 2026

The cooling loop was the first thing to go quiet. For nineteen years it ran at a pitch you could hear from the loading dock — not loud, exactly, but present, the way a refrigerator is present in a house you have lived in a long time. When the technicians drained it on the last Thursday of September, the building sounded, for the first time in anyone’s memory, like an ordinary warehouse.

Haluk Demir has worked the floor since 2007. He stood at the end of aisle four and listened to nothing, and then he went to get coffee, because there was nothing else to do yet. “You keep telling yourself it will feel like a funeral,” he said. “It feels like a move. Which is worse, somehow, because a move means you have to do the boxes.”

Signal spent four days on the floor at Meridian Ledger Services, a mid-sized clearing firm that has never been the kind of company a technology reporter calls. Its data center was never a product. It was a room with a bill. And that, according to the people who are taking it apart, is precisely why it is the next thing to go.

Section OneAisles Have a Half-Life

The Meridian room held forty-two racks at peak. By August it held nine. The shrinkage was not dramatic and was not planned as shrinkage; it was the accumulated result of eleven separate decisions, each of which looked locally sensible at the time it was made.

A storage array reached end of support and was replaced by a subscription. A disaster-recovery site in Pennsylvania was closed because a second region “covered it.” The trading co-location stayed, because the trading co-location always stays. What remained in Newark was the stuff no one had a story for: the build servers, the archive tier, the lab, and a very large tape library that nobody had admitted to still running since 2019.

“Nobody ever decided to leave. Everybody decided, one at a time, to stop arriving.”

Priya Venkatesan, Meridian’s former infrastructure director

“Nobody ever decided to leave,” said Priya Venkatesan, who ran infrastructure at Meridian until 2024 and now advises two of the firms replacing rooms like this one. “Every single migration had a business case that made sense on its own. Add them up over a decade and you have a building you are heating for nine racks.”

The arithmetic of that building is worth stating plainly, because it is the arithmetic every remaining operator is now doing. Meridian’s Newark lease ran to roughly $41 a kilowatt per month on a 340 kW contract, plus power at commercial rate, plus the standby generator service, plus the two security staff the lease required as a condition of the freight elevator. The racks inside it drew, in a good month, about 62 kW.

  • What you pay for: 340 kW of contracted capacity, whether or not you use it.
  • What you use: 62 kW, most of it in two racks that cannot be moved for licensing reasons.
  • What that works out to: about $224 per kilowatt actually consumed, against a market rate near $48 for the same load in a shared hall.

That gap is not new. What is new is that the tools for closing it stopped being frightening around 2023. Migration used to mean a project office, a consultancy, and eighteen months. At Meridian it meant two contractors, a spreadsheet with 211 rows, and a quarter.

Section TwoNine Months, on the Calendar

The closure itself is boring in the way that well-run things are boring. Signal asked the team for its working timeline and reproduced it below, with their permission and two corrections of our own.

The Closure, Month by Month

Graphic G1  ·  Source: Meridian working timeline
  1. 01

    January Decision

    The board approves the exit, not the cloud

    The resolution authorises “consolidation of owned facilities” and names no destination. Finance records it as a property decision, which is what allows it to skip the technology committee entirely.

  2. 02

    February – March Discovery

    211 circuits, and nobody owns 40 of them

    The inventory turns up forty-one cross-connects with no living document attached. Six are still carrying traffic. Two of those six are carrying traffic that nobody can explain until a vendor invoice is produced.

  3. 03

    April – May Contracting

    The clause that decided the destination

    Meridian wanted a two-year term. Its preferred hyperscaler wanted three. A carrier hotel eleven miles away offered twelve months with a month-to-month tail, and that single line item moved the whole programme.

  4. 04

    June – August Move

    Sixty-two cutovers, four of them at 3 a.m.

    Each rack became a weekend. The archive tier went first because it was the least likely to scream. The lab went last because it was the most likely to be needed and the hardest to justify keeping.

  5. 05

    September Surrender

    The loop comes down, the key goes back

    The generator service contract survives the building by four months, because its own termination notice is ninety days and nobody remembered to start the clock until the racks were already gone.

Section ThreeWhat the Contract Says

Meridian would not show us its primary migration agreement, and it is not alone: of the fourteen firms we contacted about facility closures this year, one produced a contract. What we have instead is a redacted statement of work, two people who read the original, and the invoice pattern that followed.

The pattern is consistent enough to describe. Firms leaving owned rooms rarely land on a single provider. They land on a bill with four or five names on it, only one of which is a cloud the marketing world recognises. The rest are the layer underneath: the carrier hotel, the cross-connect, the managed switch rental, the backup vendor who will happily take your tape because your tape has nowhere else to go.

Two things surprise people who have only read the analyst notes. The first is how little of the money is elastic. Compute and storage can be turned down overnight; the interconnection and support layers are annual commitments with quarterly true-ups, and they arrive whether or not you use them. The second is that the exit is now the expensive part, not the entry. Every provider wants you in. Few want to be asked what it costs to leave in month thirty-one.

  1. 1Meridian’s contracted power fell from 340 kW to zero. Its effective interconnection spend rose from $9,400 to $14,100 a month, because two sites now need the circuits one site needed.
  2. 2The twelve-month term was signed with a month-to-month tail at 1.15× the committed rate — standard language, and the reason the finance team watched it closely.
  3. 3Forty-one cancelled circuits released $6,200 a month. Six of those circuits were discovered to be load-bearing only in the final fortnight.
  4. 4The tape library was not decommissioned. It moved, and it is still running, and Meridian will not say for how long.

“We modelled it as a cost reduction,” said Demir, who stayed on through the move and is now one of three people who remain. “It is a cost reduction. It is also a change in what kind of cost it is. Before, it was a thing we had. Now it is a thing we rent, and a thing we rent can be repriced.”

Section FourThe People Who Kept the Lights On

There were eleven people on the facilities roster in January 2025. There are three now. Two took redundancy packages. Four transferred to the carrier hotel, which is eleven miles away and, for three of them, a ninety-minute change to a working day. One retired, on the day the last switch came off the wall, in a room he had worked in for nineteen years.

The transfers are the part the business cases tend to leave out, because they are not a number. A technician who has spent a decade in one building knows which breaker feeds which row without looking. That knowledge is not in the runbooks; it is in the body, and it does not survive a move intact.

Meridian says the reduction was voluntary and that no one was made redundant in the closure itself. Two former staff describe the process differently: roles were re-scoped to something they could not accept, then vacated. Both accounts can be true, and in this industry they usually are.

Section FiveWhat Replaces a Room

Here is the part the trade press keeps getting wrong. The room did not become a cloud. It became a set of arrangements. Some of Meridian’s workloads are in a hyperscaler region in Virginia. Some are in a colocation cage that its own staff still have badge access to. The archive is on media in a building in Ohio that Meridian has never visited and does not name in its own documentation.

That is not a failure of planning. It is the plan. The firms doing this well describe the goal as optionality, which is a polite word for refusing to be captured. The cost is that nobody can point at the system any more, and the further cost is that the compliance evidence has to be assembled from four vendors on four different portals in four different formats.

Venkatesan has advised six of these programmes and expects eleven more next year. Her advice to clients is unfashionable and short: write the exit down before you sign the entry. Not the migration plan — the departure plan, priced, with the notice periods and the data-egress figures and the name of the person who has to approve it.

“Every one of these rooms is somebody’s Tuesday,” she said. “The companies that do this well are the ones that accept that the next room is also going to close, and that the only real decision is whether you find out the cost of leaving before or after you need it.”

Section SixThe Last Truck

On the final afternoon, the last of the nine racks went out on a tail lift at 4:20 in the afternoon, which is the least cinematic hour for an ending. Demir locked the door himself, because the security contract had already lapsed and the code no longer worked, and he handed the physical key back through a window to a man from the landlord who did not know what he was holding.

There is a photograph of the room taken that morning, before the lift arrived. It shows a warehouse floor with a single square of tape on the concrete where aisle four used to be, and a painted line where the pallet stop used to be, and nothing else. The building is listed for lease at a rate that assumes a cooling loop.

“Somebody is going to put a data center in there,” Demir said. “Probably not a big one. Probably a firm that wants a room with a bill, and does not know yet that they are going to stop arriving one at a time.”

Method, Sources & Corrections

Transparency

  • Four days on the floor in Newark, two days at the receiving carrier hotel, and eleven recorded interviews with current and former Meridian staff. Three of those interviews were conducted on condition of anonymity because the individuals are still employed and had not been cleared to speak. Every figure in this piece was put to the company in writing; the responses we received are quoted in full in the second panel.

  • A spokesperson confirmed the closure and the reduction from eleven facilities roles to three, described both as voluntary, and disputed the characterisation of re-scoped roles. The company declined to provide the migration contract, the current interconnection spend, or the location of the archive tier. It did not dispute the contracted power figures or the $224 per kilowatt calculation, which we recomputed from the lease and twelve months of utility invoices.

  • Cost comparisons use contracted capacity rather than metered draw, because that is what the lease charges. The shared-hall benchmark of $48 per kilowatt is the median of three quotes obtained by Signal under a limited corporate identity in September 2026, for a comparable rack count and no committed burst. We have not adjusted for the free-egress promotions those quotes included.

    • 17 October 2026 — An earlier version described the standby generator contract as cancelling alongside the lease. It runs four months longer. The text has been amended.
    • 18 October 2026 — The archive tier was attributed to Ohio. Meridian says the media is held in two states, only one of which is Ohio. The sentence has been rewritten.
