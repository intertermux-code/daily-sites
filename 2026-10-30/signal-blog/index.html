The Subsea Cable Map Nobody Publishes

Fourteen thousand kilometres of fibre carry ninety-seven per cent of intercontinental traffic, and no public chart shows where they land, who owns the repeaters, or which three junctions could take half the Atlantic offline. We spent seven months rebuilding the map from cable-station logs, repair ships and one very stubborn spreadsheet.

By Marcus Oyelaran · Photographs by Nadia Ferreira

Correction: The Ledger No. 006

Published 12 Oct 2026

An earlier version gave the release year of recursive common table expressions in the standard as 1999. The relevant language appeared in SQL:1999 as an optional feature and became core in SQL:2003. The argument in the piece does not depend on the date, but the date should have been right. Our error, and thanks to three readers who wrote within the hour.

A note on our benchmark rig

Data Desk · 21 Oct 2026

Every machine-numbered claim in the Postgres piece ran on the same three hosts, with the scripts, kernel configuration and dataset checksums published alongside. Where a vendor asked us to withhold a figure we could not reproduce ourselves, we withheld it. That happened twice this quarter, in pieces you have already read.

Searching: a 1997 fibre schedule

Archives · 27 Oct 2026

We are reconstructing the permitting trail for the first privately financed transatlantic system and one document is missing — a landing-rights schedule filed with a coastal authority in the spring of 1997. If you worked at a cable head office, or inherited a box of faxes, write to the archives desk. We will credit and cite.

What we will not take

The Editor · Standing note

Signal does not accept sponsored placements, gifted hardware without disclosure, or travel paid by a company we cover. We publish our funder list and our expenses policy on the masthead page, both of them annual, both of them boring. This is not a promise about motives. It is a constraint you can check.
