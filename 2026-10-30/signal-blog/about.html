Standing Note — From the Editor-in-Chief

A Paper About Wires, Not Hype

Signal exists because the decisions that shape computing are made in places nobody covers: a landing station, a substation yard, a licence agreement, a procurement meeting with no press release. Nine people write for us. Four thousand eight hundred people read us in print. This page tells you who we are, what we owe you, and how to check both.

The Mission, In Full

Written in 2016, revised twice, and still the shortest statement of what we are for.

Technology reporting has spent a decade explaining products to consumers and markets to investors. Both are real, and neither is the interesting part. The interesting part is that computing became civilisation's infrastructure, and almost nobody is watching the infrastructure the way you would watch a bridge, a water main or a rail timetable.

We started Signal in a Lisbon back room with one rule: a dispatch is only finished when the physical or contractual thing it describes can be pointed at. A cable has a landing. A benchmark has a machine. A policy has a sentence. A company has a filing. If you cannot point at it, we do not print it, and we tell you why we could not.

That rule is expensive. It means we publish three reviews a year instead of thirty, and that a story about a data centre takes nine months rather than a news cycle. It also means our corrections are short, because the things we get wrong are usually dates rather than facts.

We are funded by readers and by nothing else. There is no advertising department to disappoint, no investor to flatter, and no parent company whose other subsidiaries we might be asked to describe gently. Our funder list is public, our expenses are published annually, and our ethics policy is on this page rather than behind a login.

If that sounds like a claim about motives, it is not meant as one. Motives are invisible. What we can show you is structure — who pays us, who we refuse money from, how a story gets cleared, and what happens when we get it wrong. Read the standards below, then read the corrections index, and decide for yourself.

Ethics & Standards

Eight rules we can be held to. Each one is checkable by a reader, which is the only kind of promise worth printing.

01

Money

No advertising, no sponsorship, no affiliate links, no platform revenue share. Ninety-one per cent of income is reader subscriptions; the remainder is foundation grants for specific reporting, each one named in our published funder list.

02

Gifts and hardware

We buy the devices we test. Loaned equipment is disclosed in the first line of any piece that mentions it, returned within thirty days, and never reviewed before we have handled a retail unit.

03

Travel and access

We pay our own way to any site we report from. Where a host covers a cost because the alternative was no access at all — a cable ship berth, a substation tour — the dispatch states it in the text.

04

Sources

Two independent sources before a claim runs, on the record wherever possible. Anonymous sourcing is approved by the Editor-in-Chief, explained to the reader, and reserved for people who lose something by speaking.

05

Corrections

Corrected at the top of the online version with a dated note, and printed in the next issue's masthead notes. Fourteen corrections since 2016, all still visible at the pieces they belong to.

06

Numbers

A machine-numbered claim publishes with its rig, its configuration and its raw logs, or it does not publish. Withdrawn pieces stay withdrawn and the reason is stated.

07

Testing and disclosure

We do not probe live systems without written authorisation, we do not publish working exploits before a patch exists, and we give vendors a dated window to respond to specifics.

08

Personal interests

Writers declare holdings, employment and advisory roles touching their beat. Two of us have current declarations; they are printed under the masthead and quoted in any piece where the interest could matter.

How a dispatch clears

Four stages · typically 6 weeks

Reporting plan approved by a desk editor; sources logged as they are taken; a fact-check pass against the log by someone who did not write the piece; then sign-off by the Editor-in-Chief. Long reads get a fifth stage: a hostile read by a practitioner in the field, paid a consultation fee we publish in the annual accounts.

What the standards board does

Seated 2022 · three members

The board is external, unpaid, and reads every dispatch after publication with a two-week window to complain. Four complaints have been upheld since 2022, each resulting in a note on the piece and one resulting in a full re-report. Board minutes are summarised annually.
