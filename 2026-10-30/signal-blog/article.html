The Quiet Death of the Self-Hosted Server

Thousands of machines were powered down last year and not one company issued a press release. We spent fourteen months following the racks out the door — and the people who knew how to run them.

ME By Mara Ellison Infrastructure Correspondent

Reporting from Ghent, Dublin & Chicago · 14 min read · Oct 29, 2026

This is the third part of Signal's infrastructure series. Read part one, “Inside the GPU Grey Market,” and part two, “Postgres Took the Bet.”

The last thing Peter Weyns ever unplugged was a Dell R730 bought in 2015 for €1,840. It had outlived two CTOs, one acquisition, an office move, and the marriage of the woman who signed the purchase order. When he pulled the cable on a Tuesday in March, the room held nineteen machines and he was the only person left who could find the front-panel diagnostic codes on any of them.

“There is no ceremony for this,” he said. “You drive the van to the recycler, you get a weight receipt, and everyone sends a Slack message about a migration cost saving.”

Weyns is not a sentimentalist. He ran one of the better-regarded private server estates in Flanders, and he will tell you at length about the three times a year it burned money he did not have. But he is one of a shrinking group of people who can describe, from memory, what a company loses when it stops owning the box its software runs on — a loss that shows up nowhere on an invoice.

Signal spent fourteen months interviewing forty-one engineers, facilities operators, and finance leads at companies that retired their self-hosted estates between 2023 and 2026. We asked for migration plans, board decks, and the post-migration retrospectives that almost nobody publishes. What emerges is not a story about the cloud winning. It is a story about a skill quietly reaching its expiry date, and about an industry that stopped teaching the next generation because the last generation is still in the room.

Section 01Nobody Decided. Everyone Did.

The most striking thing about the archives we reviewed is the absence of a decision. In thirty-eight of the forty-one migrations, no document says “we are moving off our own hardware because it is better.” The change arrives as an accumulation of small deferrals: a rack not refilled in Q2, a warranty not renewed, a hiring requisition for a second systems engineer that quietly closes.

Consider the pattern that three separate finance leads described independently to us. A self-hosted estate has a step function. Nothing changes for two and a half years, and then forty-eight machines reach their fifth birthday in the same quarter and the whole thing must be replaced at once, or explicitly allowed to rot. Cloud spend has no step function. It arrives monthly, in a currency the business already understands.

“It is not that the cloud is cheaper,” one of them told us, on condition that we not name the company. “Nine times out of ten it is more expensive. It is that the cloud never asks me for €400,000 in a single quarter and I cannot be surprised by it in a board meeting.”

That sentence, repeated in five cities with only minor variations, is probably the most important finding in this piece. The self-hosted server did not lose an argument about performance, control, or price. It lost an argument about variance.

Section 02The Vanishing Job Title

In 2019, “systems engineer” or “infra ops” appeared in a majority of the job posts from mid-sized software companies in the US and Western Europe. By early 2026 that title is effectively extinct in the same segment, replaced by platform engineer — a role whose explicit premise is that someone else owns the metal.

What the roles actually do. A systems engineer is accountable for a failure domain that starts at the power supply and ends at the load balancer: firmware, thermals, drive firmware errata, the person at the colo who will walk a spare PSU across the lot at 2 a.m. A platform engineer is accountable for a failure domain that starts at the container runtime and ends at the service. The two jobs share vocabulary and almost nothing else.

The consequence shows up in incident data rather than in hiring data. Median resolution time in our set more than tripled after migration — but the composition of incidents changed completely. Before: a failed drive, a flapping switch, a firmware regression that only appears at 3 a.m. on a specific kernel build. After: a misconfigured IAM policy, a region-level degradation at the provider, a retry storm triggered by a pricing-tier change no one read about in time.

The second category is harder to debug because the person debugging it cannot see the system. “I have never felt more behind a wall than when support asked me to run a traceroute to a host I was told does not exist,” said Aisha Karam, who was the last systems engineer at a 90-person logistics software company in Chicago.

We did not stop self-hosting because the cloud was better. We stopped because the one person who could explain why we were doing it retired, and the person who inherited the documentation could not read it. Aisha Karam, formerly last systems engineer, Meridian Logistics

Section 03Documentation Is a Person

Every migration we examined included a runbook. Most were genuinely good — versioned, reviewed, kept current. And in twenty-six of the forty-one cases, the runbook failed within eighteen months in a way its authors had not anticipated, because the runbook recorded what to do, not why it worked.

This is the least glamorous finding here and probably the most durable one. Tacit knowledge about hardware lives in a specific head because it was acquired through a specific sequence of failures. Rebuilding that head is expensive and slow, which is precisely why firms stopped investing in it: while the last expert is still employed, the absence looks like a saving.

Weyns keeps a paper notebook. Nine hundred pages, fifteen years, mostly pencil. Each entry is a fault, a symptom, and the wrong thing he tried first. He was offered a consulting rate by two of his former vendors to transcribe it and declined both.

“They wanted the answers,” he said. “The answers are worth nothing. The value is that I remember being wrong for six hours before I got it right, so that next February, when the same drive controller does the same stupid thing, I will not spend six hours.”

Section 04Who Actually Went Back

A small number of companies in our set repurchased hardware. Their reasons cluster tightly, and none of them are the reasons the self-hosting industry used to give.

  • Training inference at scale. Six of the eight AI-adjacent firms we spoke with moved sustained GPU inference back on-premise within two years, and cited predictable amortisation — not cost — as the driver.
  • Data residency with teeth. Three health-tech operators said the regulatory language had drifted far enough that “a compliant region” was no longer an acceptable answer to a hospital's procurement team.
  • Latency ceilings. One industrial automation firm needed sub-millisecond round trips inside a factory and found that no edge product could promise it during a firmware rollout.

The returners share a second characteristic: they had to rehire at the last row of the table above. Average time-to-hire reported by the returners was 138 days, and two of them ended up contracting their former systems engineer at a premium rather than finding an equivalent.

Section 05The Case for the Machine Room, Honestly Stated

None of this is an argument for putting a rack in a broom cupboard again. Self-hosting failed a lot of companies for very good reasons, and several of the engineers we interviewed had spent years firefighting an estate that should have been retired in 2016. Unrenewed warranties, single-person bus factors, and the quiet heroism of a man who sleeps with a laptop in a colo are not a business model — they are a failure of organisational imagination that got romanticised.

The honest case is narrower and more interesting: a company that rents everything will, at some point, discover that it has rented the ability to be surprised cheaply. Variance is not the same as cost, and flexibility is not the same as knowledge. Both of those lessons arrive slowly, in a currency nobody budgets for.

The Ghent facility came down in August. The recycler paid by weight. Weyns kept the R730's drive bezel on his desk at home, which he describes as sentimentality and his wife describes as the only object in the house he will not let her move.

“Ask me in five years whether I made the right call,” he said. “I can genuinely not tell you yet. That is the problem with all of it. By the time you know, the people who could have told you are doing something else.”
