The Subsea Cable Map Nobody Publishes

Nine months, forty-one sources on the record, and a spreadsheet nobody was supposed to keep: how we rebuilt the chart of the cables that carry almost everything, and what it shows about the three places the Atlantic is narrow enough to break.

The most consequential document in the submarine cable industry is a spreadsheet that its own maintainers will not describe. It is not secret in the way a cipher is secret. It is secret in the way a lease is secret: dozens of companies hold pieces of it, each piece is commercially sensitive, and no single party has both the standing and the appetite to publish the whole. What exists in public instead is a set of pretty charts — colour-coded arcs across ocean basins, drawn at a scale that makes the seabed look like a dinner plate — and every engineer I spoke to for this story had the same reaction to those charts. They laughed, and then they told me the arcs were wrong.

The claim in the title of this piece is narrow and checkable. There is no public, authoritative, per-element map of the submarine fibre systems that carry intercontinental traffic: not of their branching units, not of the repeaters between them, not of which operator holds which pair of fibres inside which cable, and not of the spare capacity that makes a route survivable. The International Telecommunication Union maintains a list of cable systems and their landing points.1 Private consortia publish route diagrams for their own systems. Researchers at a handful of universities have assembled approximations from public filings. Nobody has published the assembly that matters — the one that says: this fibre, in this cable, crosses this branching unit, and terminates at this room, where these four companies share a rack.

We spent nine months trying to build it, and the exercise taught me more about how the internet actually works than six years of covering its applications. The internet is usually described as a graph of autonomous systems and a protocol that finds paths. That is true of the part you can see from a capital city. Between capital cities, it is a set of very expensive physical agreements, and the routing software is downstream of the contract.

Section 01The Chart That Is Not a Chart

Start with the thing everybody cites. Public cable maps are drawn as great-circle arcs, because that is what a cartographer can do with a line and an ocean. Real cables do not follow great circles. They follow the seafloor, and the seafloor follows the rules of what can be laid without the cable snapping under its own weight in a trench or breaking on a slope.

Submarine cable engineers talk about this as a gradient problem. A system's profile is planned so that the descent never exceeds a certain angle, which means routes bend around seamounts and canyon heads, and double back in ways that look like mistakes on a flat map. One route planner told me, over the phone in Brest, that the difference between the arc the public sees and the cable on the seabed can be more than four hundred kilometres in an Atlantic crossing.2 That is not a rounding error. That is a different country's worth of distance, and it changes which repair ship can reach the cable, which fishing grounds the crossing passes through, and where the traffic will actually be when a fault occurs.

Then there is the branching problem, which no published map shows at all. Modern trunk systems are not point-to-point. A single cable between two coasts carries branching units — repeatered spur junctions on the seabed — that peel off capacity toward a third or fourth landing. To the traffic on land it looks like one system. On the seabed it is a tree. When a fault occurs upstream of a branch, everything on that branch loses service together, and the operator at the far end, who may not even be a shareholder in the cable, is downstream of a fault in somebody else's wire.

“You keep asking me who owns the cable. Nobody owns the cable. Twelve companies own pairs of fibres, and one of them owns a landing station, and the maintenance agreement is what holds that together like a mortar.”

Consortium manager, transatlantic system — on the record

The first time I heard that sentence, I wrote it down as a shrug. It is not a shrug. It is the load-bearing fact of this story. Ownership of a cable is a legal fiction maintained by a document called a maintenance agreement, and the maintenance agreement allocates liability for faults by reference to which segment of the system failed — segments that, absent a published map, the parties themselves describe using internal drawings.

Section 02Where the Glass Comes Ashore

Landing stations are the part of the network that citizens pass without noticing. A submarine cable comes ashore through a duct under a beach or a rocky headland, runs to a building the size of a house, and stops. Everything seaward of that building is a different physical world: no routers, no software, no IP addresses. Just lasers, fibre, and repeaters powered by a direct current sent through the copper sheath at up to twenty thousand volts, with the whole loop's current set by whichever operator is drawing the most.3

The building is where the map becomes a floor plan. Inside, each operator's terminals sit on a rack, and between the racks sits the patch panel that lets capacity be rerouted on land. That panel — sometimes hand-labelled, sometimes a spreadsheet of jack numbers maintained by the station's landlord — is the single most valuable and least published document in the industry, because it determines whether an operator at one landing can move traffic to another landing at all.

Getting photographs inside these buildings took four months and one fortunate accident: the technician who agreed to walk me through a station on the Portuguese coast was the same person who had, two years earlier, replied to a reader's public question about power feed arrangements. He asked me, plainly, whether publishing the floor plan would help anyone attack it. I said no, and he agreed, and we spoke for two hours about the things that are already obvious from space — the duct route, the beach, the number of vehicles outside — and the one thing that is not: how many independent power supplies serve the room.

The answer, at most stations we could confirm, is one utility feed and a diesel reserve, and the reserve is tested on a schedule maintained by a facilities contractor who is not the cable operator. Three operators told me independently that they consider the generator, not the cable, the worst single-point risk in their chain. That is a sentence you will not find in any public map.

Section 03Ownership by a Thousand Small Agreements

Cable systems are financed the way a railway was financed in the nineteenth century: a consortium signs a contract before the steel exists, and the contract defines who gets what fraction of a thing that will lie on the bottom of an ocean for twenty-five years. The industry calls these the letters of agreement, and they are not published. I obtained, reconstructed or had described to me the commercial shape of nineteen systems.

From the registry — note 4

Of the nineteen systems whose commercial shape we could reconstruct, thirteen allocate capacity by pair of fibres rather than by wavelength, six have a single member holding a veto over route changes because that member controls the landing, and two list a party in the agreement that no longer exists as a company under that name.

That last category is not a clerical joke. A cable is a legal object with a longer lifespan than the corporate form of the people who bought it. Two of the systems we traced had landing rights held by entities that were absorbed, renamed, or split during the merger wave of the early 2020s, and the successor in each case could not produce, to my satisfaction, a document establishing that the obligation transferred. When I asked a lawyer who advises several consortia about this, he used the word “tidemark” and then declined to be quoted, because he had clients on both sides of an unresolved instance of it.4

Why does anyone accept this? Because the alternative is worse. Consortium construction is the only mechanism that has ever produced enough cable to meet demand at a price the market will pay, and it works precisely because it lets a small carrier buy a pair of fibres on a system it does not control, rather than having to build its own crossing. The opacity is not a bug in the model. It is the price of the model.

Section 04Three Junctions

Reconstructing the routing produces one useful, alarming output: the places where a small number of physical objects stand between a large share of traffic and its destination. Regulators have a phrase for these — single points of critical dependence — and they use it loosely. Our dataset lets you be less loose about it.

  • Corridor where two coasts come within a shelf narrowing, and every system in the basin crosses it1 corridor
  • Shore-end junctions where more than sixty per cent of a region's outbound capacity shares a single building3 sites
  • Branching units, seaward of those buildings, whose upstream fault would take two national routes dark simultaneously7 units
  • Independent repair ships capable of splicing a repeatered segment anywhere in the world11 ships
  • Days of notice, in our survey, before a ship can reach a fault in the North Atlantic in winter10–21

The three shore-end sites are not exotic locations. They are the ordinary, well-documented cable heads that everyone in the industry already knows about, and the reason they matter is not secrecy but density: the same beach, the same duct bank, the same road, and in one case the same substation plinth, serving systems that were sold to customers as geographically diverse routes. A customer's protected service and its “diverse” backup can share a metre of trench. This is not hypothetical. It has happened, in the last decade, more than once, and each time the explanation offered afterwards was about route documentation.

Seven branching units is a small number for a continent. It is also a number you can do something about: they are already on the seabed, and the operators know exactly where they are. The argument for publishing is not that a state would go and dig them up. It is that planners, insurers, cloud architects and regulators currently reason about resilience using a map that cannot express the risk, and then write contracts that assume the reasoning was sound.

Section 05The Economics of Repair

A cable fault is not an outage; it is a logistics event. The ship has to be free, the spare must be on board, the weather window has to hold, and the repair has to be coordinated with every other party whose traffic crosses the fault. There are fifty-three cable ships in the world and, by the count three operators and two industry bodies gave me, eleven that can splice a repeatered segment. The fleet is privately owned, several vessels are decades old, and at least two are chartered by the same operator for two basins at once.

“We do not sell you resilience. We sell you a repair priority. If you want the priority, it is a line item in the maintenance agreement, and it is priced like a line item.”

Fibre capacity broker, London — not for attribution

That sentence reframed the last month of my reporting. Repair priority is a commercial instrument, and it is invisible to anyone buying transit at retail. When a large carrier tells a customer that its routes are protected, the protection may be a fast ship rather than a second path — and the fast ship is not guaranteed to be in the ocean.5

The insurance market knows this. Two underwriters described pricing sub-sea risk as a problem of correlating exposures they cannot see, which is why premiums on shore-end concentration are rising in a market where the physical asset has not changed. The risk that moved is the disclosure.

Section 06What the Map Changes

Nobody is going to publish the whole thing, and I do not think the case for publishing the whole thing has been made. Landing station floor plans and duct routes are genuine security surfaces, and the operators who raised this were not being lazy; they were being responsible. The interesting proposal is narrower and, to me, obviously achievable: a shared registry of attributes rather than positions — which systems share a shore-end, which share a duct bank, which cross which, and which branching units sit upstream of more than one national route. No coordinates. No floor plans. Just the graph of co-dependence, which is the part that is currently unknowable to everyone except the parties, and often not to them.

Two national regulators have told me they would accept such a registry if the industry built it. Three operators told me they would contribute if their competitors did. That is the whole of the political problem, and it is the kind of problem that does not resolve itself while everyone waits politely for someone else to move first.

What I took from nine months of it is less policy-minded than that. We have built a set of arrangements in which the physical layer of the global internet is governed by private documents, priced by private contracts, and maintained by eleven ships, and then designed an applications layer on top that behaves as though transport were a utility with no geography. The map is not the territory. But the territory is there, and the glass is on the bottom of the sea, and the only people who know exactly where it lies have agreed not to say.
