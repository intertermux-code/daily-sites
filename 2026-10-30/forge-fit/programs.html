Anatomy of a Forge block

12 weeks · 4 phases

A block is twelve weeks of planned work with one measurable goal at the end. Coaches write the whole block before week one starts, which is why the board tells you what you are doing instead of asking you to invent it while tired.

  1. Weeks 1–3 · Base. Volume up, weight down. Positions get grooved, work capacity gets built.
  2. Weeks 4–7 · Build. Sets drop, load climbs. This is where most of the strength is made.
  3. Weeks 8–10 · Peak. Specific work at competition pace, with attempts rehearsed under fatigue.
  4. Week 11 · Deload. Half the tonnage, none of the ego. You come back sharper, not softer.
  5. Week 12 · Test. New numbers, a video review with your coach, and the next block written from what we learned.

01

Powerlifting

Squat · Bench · Deadlift

This is the program the building was rebuilt for. You train the back squat, bench press and deadlift two or three times a week with accessory work chosen for your weak point — not for variety.

Complete beginners start on an empty 20 kg bar with a coach at their side and add load only when the position holds. Experienced lifters bring their last total and we build from the openers. Between those two extremes, everyone runs the same wave: heavy day, volume day, and a third session of technique under fatigue.

Every six weeks the club runs an intra-gym meet on the Thursday platforms. Commands are called, judges are seated, attempts are logged. You do not have to enter it — but most people who train here end up wanting to.

Find a conditioning class

Intervals with a purpose and a number attached. The coach publishes the target — say 240 watts average for eight 250 m rows — and the monitor tells you whether you hit it. No music-loud mystery circuits, no scoreboard shame.

Sessions rotate across four engines: the 30 m sled track, the row, the assault bike, and barbell complexes that keep the heart rate up while the hands stay busy. Warm-ups are eight minutes of genuine preparation, then the work starts on the clock.

Every class has a scaled version written into the same plan. If you are returning after years off, you take the shorter interval and the lighter sled; the structure is identical, and the coach checks your recovery heart rate at the two-minute mark rather than the highlight reel.

03

Mobility & Rebuild

Range you can load

Flexibility without strength at end range is a hole you will eventually fall into. This class builds both: controlled articular rotations, loaded stretching, breath work and single-leg stability, in a 45-minute session you can do on a rest day.

It is also where return-to-training happens. If you have been off for six months, coming from physiotherapy, or managing a shoulder that catches, you start here with Priya and her team. You will be screened, given a written progression, and moved onto a barbell class when the positions hold — not before.

Most members run one rebuild class per week alongside two lifting days. Lifters in a peak block take it the day after heavy deadlifts, which is the cheapest recovery tool we own.

  • Ankle and hip range drills that change how your squat looks within two weeks.
  • Thoracic and shoulder work for anyone who sits eight hours a day.
  • Nasal breathing and down-regulation to finish every session.
  • A printed home sheet — eight minutes, three times a week, no equipment.

06

Questions before you pick

Six we get at the desk
I have never lifted. Which program?

Start with one Mobility & Rebuild class and two Powerlifting classes in your first fortnight. The rebuild session shows you the positions we expect; the platform sessions teach you the bar. Nobody will put you under a heavy load until you can hold a brace on your own.

Can I do all three in one week?

Yes, and most of our stronger members do. The usual shape is two lifting days, one conditioning day and one rebuild day. Unlimited covers every class on the board, so the only limit is sleep.

What if I miss a week?

Tell your coach and the block flexes. Missing up to seven days means you resume at the same load; longer than that and we repeat the previous week rather than pushing into a session you are not ready for.

Do I have to compete?

No. Roughly one in five powerlifting members enters a meet. The rest want the structure, the testing day and a bigger number they can keep to themselves.

Is the conditioning hard for someone with asthma or a bad knee?

Tell us on the intake form and the coach writes you an alternative engine — usually the bike instead of the sled, or shorter intervals with longer rest. Conditioning classes run scaled lines on the board every single session.

Can I transfer programming if I travel?

Your block is written in your account, printable as a one-page sheet, and every session lists hotel-gym substitutions for the accessory work. Ask a coach for the travel version before you fly.
