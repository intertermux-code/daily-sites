A Shop Built Out of Leftovers

We bought a printing works and inherited its arguments.

Section One

How It Started

Marguerite Okonjo had eleven years of bookselling in other people’s shops and a redundancy cheque with her name spelled wrong on it.

In the spring of 1994 she took a lease on a former printing works on Warren Street because it had three things: a freight lift, a stove in the back wall, and a floor strong enough for shelving. The firm that left had gone under mid-job, and the building came with four crates of unclaimed proofs — novels with the wrong title on the cover, a gardening manual printed twice, and one anthology nobody could identify. We still sell three of them. Two we refuse to price.

The name came from a joke in the proofs. A printer had set the running head of a poem as “Chapter & Verse” when it should have read “Chapter and Verse,” and the & had been left there because it fit. Thirty-two years later the ampersand is still on the sign and still the only typographical decision nobody has regretted.

We have never taken investment, never stocked a title because a distributor pushed it, and never closed on a Thursday. The shop has been bankrupt twice on paper and has never once been without a book you could ask for.

Section Two

Thirty-Two Years, Abbreviated

The short version of a long argument with a building.

  1. 1994

    The lease, the stove, the proofs

    Opening day, 3 September: nine hundred books, one customer who came back every day that week, and a bell Ellis’s predecessor installed so badly it still sticks in frost.

  2. 1998

    The rarity room opens downstairs

    Theo Lindqvist joins to run a stock nobody in the shop had touched: forty cases bought from an estate in Rhinebeck, including the Dracula first with the cracked hinge. Eight steps down, 18 degrees, and the best-lit wrong decision we ever made.

  3. 2004

    The book club, started by accident

    Six readers turned up on the same Thursday to ask whether the shop ran a club. We did not, so Marguerite said yes, made tea, and put a ledger by the door. Two hundred and eighteen names later, the ledger is on its fourth volume.

  4. 2011

    We knock through to the passage

    The children’s loft goes up the passage, the lift comes out of the freight shaft and into service, and the long room gains eleven feet of shelf. The wall we broke had a 1911 notice behind it about a lost dog; we framed it instead of throwing it out.

  5. 2019

    The flood, and what came out of it

    Fourteen inches of Warren Street water in March. Two hundred ruined books, three hundred saved in a weekend by people who had never met each other. The drying racks upstairs are still there, and the rebinding bin still says thank you on the side.

  6. 2026

    Thirty-two thousand titles and four staff

    Same stove, same ampersand, same rule: read it before you recommend it. The poetry wall moved beside the stair, the late night stays late, and we are hiring for the first time since 2017.

Section Three

Who You Will Meet at the Counter

Four people, thirty-one years of service between them, and a standing rule that every recommendation must come from a book the recommender actually finished.

Section Four

Four Rules We Do Not Break

Read it first

No staff card on the shelf unless somebody in this building finished the book. It costs us a faster rotation and it is the only reason anyone trusts us.

Tell the truth about condition

A cracked hinge in the description saves a walk back and a lost customer. We have written ourselves out of sales with those words and would write them again.

Keep the difficult books

The slow, the strange, the out-of-print and the unfashionable. If we only stocked what sold easily, there would be nowhere left to find the rest of it.

The room is free

Readings, arguments, story hour, and a chair at any hour of the day. Nobody has to buy anything to sit down, and we have never asked.

What has been written about us

“The last shop in the county where a bookseller will talk you out of a book rather than sell it to you.”

Hudson Valley Magazine, 2024 — alongside the more useful line that we are “a library that takes your money by mistake.” We have both printed on the wall by the till, framed, at Ellis’s insistence, and they are correct.

Section Five

Come and Argue With Us

The front door on Warren Street is unlocked from nine on Fridays. The kettle is on from ten. Ask for the book you think we will not have.

We will find it, order it, or tell you honestly why we do not stock it — and we will write your name on a card so the shelf person knows which argument to hand you next. That is the entire business model.
