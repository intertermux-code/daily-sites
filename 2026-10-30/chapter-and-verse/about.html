Section VI — The Shop

Fifteen Years in a Letterpress Shop

We took on a room full of ink-stained floorboards in 2011 because none of us could get a book we liked into the town’s other two shops on purpose.

214 Marlowe Street, Hudson The press is still in the back room, still works, and still prints the club programmes badly on purpose.

How It Started, and Why It Stayed

Told by the three of us who were there, in whichever order we disagree

In the autumn of 2010, Nadia Ellery was working four days a week in a chain store thirty minutes up the river and spending her staff discount on books she was not allowed to order. She, Owen Pike and a bookbinder named Marcus Fee had been going for three years to a reading group that met in a church basement, and in October that basement got sold. They needed somewhere to put the books, and then — because they were fools — somewhere to put the tables.

The unit on Marlowe Street had been a letterpress shop since 1898 and a print-repair business until 2006. It still had the presses, a floor coated in a century of ink, and a back staircase nobody would have chosen for a public building. The landlord, who was seventy-one and had never once wanted the space back, offered eight hundred dollars a month on the condition that we kept the presses and did not paint the floor. We have honoured both.

We opened in March 2011 with about nine hundred titles, a card index, and no till — the counter was a door on trestles until June. The first month we sold forty-one books. The first Thursday reading, in November 2011, had six attendees, four of whom were each other’s family. By 2014 the readings were filling upstairs and the card index had become a shelf list that we still print annually, badly, on the press.

The thing that kept us open was not taste, which is cheap. It was the decision, made in our second year and never revisited, that we would never take a publisher’s money to place a book somewhere it did not belong. We lost a distributor for four months over it. We also got the two customers who now bring us their grandchildren, and a town that tells its own children what to read here rather than online.

Fifteen years on there are nine of us, two thousand one hundred and forty titles, four book club circles, one cat named Verity who was adopted off the poetry shelf in 2019, and a rule on the ordering desk that reads: Would we sell it to somebody who trusts us? The answer decides the shelf.

Fifteen Years, Selected

A partial ledger of the shop, in order

  1. 2011

    Doors Open in March

    Nine hundred titles, a door on trestles for a till, and forty-one books sold in the first month. The presses stay; the floor does not get painted.

  2. 2012

    The First Thursday Reading

    Six people, four of them related. It has run every term-time Thursday since, with only two cancellations: one flood and one illness.

  3. 2013

    Book Club Becomes Four Circles

    The basement group needs a permanent home. Twelve readers become sixty across the Long Novel Club, Poetry & Argument, Gothic Late and, later, Speculative Sundays.

  4. 2015

    We Refuse the Table Money

    A distributor offers good money for a front window position. We say no, lose the account for four months, and gain the rule still printed on the ordering desk.

  5. 2017

    The Back Room Goes Child-Height

    Aoife Brennan builds a low shelf and a cushion corner out of the crate stack. The Children’s Hour begins fortnightly and never stops growing.

  6. 2019

    Verity Arrives Off the Poetry Shelf

    Also the year the letterpress programme starts: every club season gets set, printed and pinned by the till, and half of it is illegible and treasured.

  7. 2020

    Door Chain, Porch Library, Phone Club

    We shelve a third of the stock on the pavement, run a lending porch, and telephone the Housebound circle every night for nineteen weeks. Nobody missed a book.

  8. 2023

    The Condemnations Box Opens

    Five dollars buys a rejected title out of the pile. It has since paid for ninety-one author visits, two kettle replacements and a new roof gutter.

  9. 2026

    Season Eleven, and Fifteen Years

    Two thousand one hundred and forty titles in stock, nine booksellers, one hundred and eighteen readings, and the same floor nobody is allowed to paint.

Nine People, Five Bays

Whoever is on the counter will tell you which shelf is theirs

NE

Nadia Ellery

Owner · Poetry & the Late Shift

Started the shop after being told twice that a poetry section was “not commercial for this town.” Runs the Long Novel Club and the condemnation box, and has an opinion about the Owen you should be prepared for.

OP

Owen Pike

Co-owner · Gothic, Bay Four

Reads the whole of Dracula aloud every October without notes, which is either a gift or a medical event. Handles the archive box of broadsides and the shop’s only working ladder.

TM

Theodore Marsh

Reader in Residence · Bay Five

Retired English teacher, forty-one years of classrooms, now employed to lose arguments at a counter. Hosts Poetry & Argument and the archive nights, and writes the shelf notes in pencil.

PR

Priya Raman

Buyer · Speculative, Bay Three

Orders two thousand one hundred and forty titles a year and remembers every one she said no to. Runs Speculative Sundays and the translation clinic, and shelve-by-length is her fault entirely.

AB

Aoife Brennan

Events & the Back Room

Built the child-height shelving from crate wood in 2017 and has run the Children’s Hour since. Keeps the rota, the seating plans, and the forty-year record of who forgave Edmund.

MF

Marcus Fee

Bookbinder · Workshop, Saturdays

Third of the three who started it. Repairs what the shop sells and teaches eight people at a time to sew a signature, on the bench where the presses used to stand.

Also on the rota: Junie Alvarez (until she finishes her degree), Callum Reyes (Saturday mornings, philosophy), and Verity, who is not on the rota and has never been asked.

What We Shelve By

Five rules, written on the ordering desk in 2013, unchanged

I

Nobody Pays for a Position

No publisher money moves a book in this shop. A window display is an opinion, and opinions here are free to everybody and expensive to us.

II

We Sell the Edition We Would Read

Translation, text, paper and spine all count. When we stock two versions of the same poem we will tell you which one we keep for ourselves.

III

The Room Is Free Until It Isn’t

Readings cost nothing. Workshops cost what the materials cost. The upstairs room is open to local groups on Mondays, with the kettle full.

IV

Honesty Over the Sale

If you can get it cheaper elsewhere, or should not buy it at all, we will say so before the till. It has never once cost us a customer who mattered.

V

Everybody Gets to Read

Housebound members get a book posted and a phone call on the night. Nobody in this county is excluded from a circle because of a staircase.

Come to Marlowe Street

Directions, doors, and the things worth knowing first

Twenty minutes from the city by train, then a nine-minute walk from Hudson station down Warren to Marlowe. The front door is level and unlocked from nine on weekdays; the bell above it was fitted in 2011 and has not been serviced or silenced.

Parking is free behind the firehouse on Warren and on the street after six. There is a ramp round the side of the building, and if you need the front room rather than eleven steps, say so at the till and we will move the whole event downstairs. Bring a bag you do not mind filling with books.
