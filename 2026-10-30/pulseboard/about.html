01 The story so far

Seven years of refusing to move the data

  1. 2019

    A funnel that lied by omission

    Ilse Vermeer and Dario Bisset ran growth at Northwind Freight. Their analytics vendor sampled anything over 10 million rows, which conveniently excluded the 214 enterprise accounts driving 31% of revenue. They wrote a query directly against the warehouse over a long weekend and had the real answer in an afternoon.

  2. 2020

    First engine, first refusal

    The prototype became a product with one rule: never copy the raw events. Fourteen teams joined the beta. Two of them were told by their vendors that warehouse-native analytics was a demo gimmick that would never survive production scale. It survived on a 41 million-row table in Rotterdam.

  3. 2021

    The metrics layer, added under protest

    We shipped boards fast and watched customers build the same “active account” in four different ways. Adding a governed definition layer felt like scope creep until a customer’s board meeting turned into a debate about a filter clause. It became the feature teams cite most.

  4. 2022

    1,000 teams and a security team that said yes

    SOC 2 Type II, the Lisbon office, and the first bank customer whose information-security review took two hours instead of two quarters. Read-only access to their own warehouse turned out to be the argument that closed deals nobody else could reach.

  5. 2023

    Anomaly watches, and profitability

    Alerting started as an internal tool to catch our own pipeline regressions. Customers asked for it, so we built seasonality-aware bands and automatic dimension drill-down. The company also went cash-flow positive without raising the round we had assumed we would need.

  6. 2024

    Nine regions, zero egress defaults

    Residency became a product decision rather than a legal document. EU, US and APACP control planes, BYOK on Nucleus, and a published statement of exactly what leaves a customer’s network: the result set they asked for, nothing else.

  7. 2025

    dbt import and the retention scan rewrite

    Ingesting an existing dbt manifest removed the last “we would have to rebuild our models” objection. Rewriting retention scans made our most expensive query type 2.4× cheaper, which matters more than another chart type.

  8. 2026

    4,200 teams, still no data copy

    Work now: experiment reading with sequential tests, a cohort overlap matrix, and making the honest trade-offs in our documentation as good as the product. If a smaller tool fits you better, we would rather say so in the demo than churn you in month five.

02 Letter from the founders

Why we still say no to the easy architecture

The obvious way to build analytics is to ingest a copy of your events into a fast column store you own. It is cheaper for us, it makes latency guarantees trivial, and it would sell. We have turned it down every year since 2019 because the copy is where trust goes to die.

Once your events live in two places, they disagree. Then someone reconciles, and the reconciliation is a third source of truth with no owner. Keeping the query where the data already is means there is exactly one number to argue about, and your warehouse team can see the SQL that produced it.

A metric you cannot audit is an opinion with a chart attached.

Ilse Vermeer Co-founder & CEO · with Dario Bisset, CTO

What we optimise for instead: a query planner that respects your compute budget, a metrics layer with a review trail, and documentation that says what a feature is bad at. It is slower to build and it makes our infrastructure bill somebody else’s — which is exactly the point.

04 Operating principles

Five rules we can be held to

01

One number, one owner

Every metric has a person whose name is on it. If two charts disagree, that is a bug with a ticket, not a meeting.

02

Show the SQL

Anything the product computes must be inspectable in the language your warehouse team already reads. No black boxes, no private dialect.

03

Publish the trade-off

Every feature page states what it costs in compute and where it is weak. We would rather lose the deal than inherit the surprise.

04

Never copy the events

The founding constraint. It costs us margin and it costs us latency headroom, and it is still the right answer.

05

Say “stay put” when it fits

Roughly one in nine demos ends with us recommending a lighter tool. Those teams come back later with a bigger warehouse and no resentment.

06

Write the incident down

Customer-facing incidents get a public retrospective with a timeline, within five working days, including the version we should have caught it in.
