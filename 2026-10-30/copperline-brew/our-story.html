We Bought the Building Nobody Wanted

A copper wire-drawing plant closed on Foundry Street in 2001. Thirteen years later we signed a lease on it because the crawl spaces were already the right size for fermentation tanks — and we have never once tried to make it look like anything else.

Chapter 01 1911–2014

Two People, One Bad Loan

Marcus Reyes ran facilities for a logistics company and had spent four years walking the back rooms of derelict industrial property. Dana Whitlock had homebrewed on a stove in a third-floor walkup until the day the apartment stopped allowing open flame — so she quit the office job instead.

They met at a homebrew club meeting held in a VFW hall in Hamtramck. Six months later they signed a ten-year lease on 11,400 square feet with a leaking roof, and spent their savings on a used 12-barrel stack that arrived in nine pieces on a flatbed.

Twelve years, in order

The Log Book

Everything below is pulled from the same binder that hangs by the brewhouse door, including the years that did not go well.

  1. 13

    Keys, and a roof that leaked in four places

    We bought the building with a loan we should not have qualified for, then spent the first summer on ladders. The copper bus bar in the east wall still worked, so we left it. Nine thousand dollars of that summer went to a three-phase electrical upgrade that let the malt mill run at all.

  2. 14

    First pint, and the amber that never changed

    Batch № 001 was Rolling Mill Amber, mashed on 8 November 2014. We poured it to about four hundred people at a soft-open on a Tuesday. The recipe is on batch № 1,284 with two hop substitutions and nothing else different.

  3. 16

    Wirecutter wins, and we immediately ruin it

    A state medal put the IPA on the map and doubled our Friday nights. Then we tried to scale it on a bigger dry-charge schedule and lost a full tank to over-extraction bitterness. The recipe we reverted to is the one still on the wall.

  4. 18

    The cellar year: barrels, lacto, and a glycol failure

    We built the barrel program in the crawl space under the old drawing line, and started kettle-souring Patina with a house lacto culture. In July the glycol chiller died in a 96°F week and we lost eight tanks. FV-09 and the second chiller are the direct result.

  5. 21

    Ten thousand hours, and the year the crew bought in

    The first barleywine barreling went in during a closed taproom, and the patio became the entire business for a summer. At year end, ownership moved to an employee trust: nine of us now hold equity and we vote on the holiday schedule.

  6. 23

    The Wire gets its second lauter ring

    We finally replaced the manual rake with a powered arm, doubled the brite capacity to six vessels, and put the canner in the room where the wire spools used to cool. Output went from 480 to 900 barrels a year without adding a tank to the brewhouse.

  7. 26

    Where the board stands today

    Twelve rotating beers, ten house lines, four guest lines, thirty-one hundred barrels through 2026, and one building we would still rather fix than finish. Batch № 1,284 is in dry hop right now, and the Dec 3 release is the biggest bottling run we have ever attempted.

Four Things We Will Not Trade Away

01 Grain

Sixty Miles, Maximum

Two-row and rye from a malt house outside Armada, hops contracted out of the Yakima Valley two years ahead. We would rather pay more and know the field.

02 Water

City Supply, Edited

We reverse-osmose and rebuild mineral content per style rather than pretend our tap water suits every beer. The pilsner gets the lightest build we run.

03 Waste

Nothing Goes to Landfill

Spent grain to a dairy farm weekly, hops to a composter, glasses reclaimed. Heat from the glycol chiller runs the taproom floor in January.

04 Pace

We Do Not Chase Drops

Fourteen beers a year is what one stack and ten tanks can hold honestly. A beer that misses spec gets poured out, and we write the reason in the binder.

Left in on purpose

Things Still in the Room From 1911

Five details we could have removed and did not. Find them on your next visit — we will point if you ask.

The bus bar

Copper, Still Hot

A four-inch copper bus bar runs the east wall. It fed the drawing motors; now it feeds the control panel, and Marcus still touches it to check load.

The line pad

Bolt Holes in Concrete

The FV stand sits on the footprint of the original wire line. You can see the shadow of where the spool frames were, cast into the slab.

The payrolls

148 Names, Under the Floor

Ledgers from 1911 to 1968, found under the office boards. We transcribed them, framed the best page, and named a beer after a shift foreman on it.

The skylights

North Light Only

Original wired glass, replaced but not resized. Hop oils degrade in sunlight, so every tank room keeps its light indirect — which is also why it looks the way it does.

The yard gate

Rail, Not Decor

The steel gate at Foundry Street is a section of the plant’s old internal rail. It still rolls on its original track, which is the only reason it is heavy enough to stay open with your hip.
