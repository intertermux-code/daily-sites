01How it started

Forty minutes of arguing about a definition.

In the autumn of 2021, Dana Okafor was head of product at a 180-person logistics software company. Her Monday review began, as usual, with a disagreement that was not about the business.

Growth reported activation at 14 percent. Finance reported 11.2. Both numbers were correct, calculated honestly, from the same warehouse — one counted accounts that completed a key action in seven days, the other in fourteen, and nobody had decided which one the company believed. The meeting spent its first twenty minutes on that gap and its last twenty on nothing else.

Dana and Tomas Reinholt, then her analytics engineer, wrote the rule down on one page: a metric is a versioned object with an owner, an exclusion list, and a review date. Boards became views of those objects rather than copies of a query. When the following Monday started at the actual question, they quit within the year and started building it properly.

Five years on, that one-page idea is still the centre of the product. Everything else — the rollups, the decision ledger, the alerts that inherit their thresholds from a definition — exists to keep the shared truth fast enough and close enough at hand that people actually use it in the room.

2023

Made it fast, on purpose

A customer presented a board to their board of directors and the chart took eleven seconds to appear on the projector. That embarrassment became a written requirement: p95 under two seconds measured in the browser, published monthly, and revisited whenever a pull request threatens it.

  • Incremental rollups shipped in March
  • Latency budget moved into the CI pipeline
  • First EU-resident workspace onboarded

2024

Gave it a memory

Teams told us the same thing: the numbers were finally trustworthy, and the meeting still ended without a decision. So boards learned to record outcomes — who raised it, what was chosen, which metric will prove it. The decision ledger is the reason Monday starts with last Monday.

  • Point-anchored comments replaced page threads
  • Automatic outcome annotations on timelines
  • Weekly digest generated from decisions, not charts

05How we work

Four principles we are willing to be judged on.

Ask us about one→

01 / Truth over speed

A number says when it was measured

Every figure carries its as-of time and its freshness rule. If a tail is behind, the board admits it in words rather than quietly showing you yesterday.

02 / Fewer objects

We remove features on purpose

Two release notes this year removed more than they added. If a control needs a paragraph of explanation, it becomes a default or it disappears.

03 / Your data stays yours

We are a reader, not a custodian

Warehouse-first by design. Exports are free, the metric model leaves as YAML, and nothing about leaving should cost you a quarter of engineering time.

04 / Publish the bad days

Incidents and latency, in public

Ninety days of status history, monthly latency percentiles, and a written rollback plan per release. Trust is a record, not a promise.
