The Quiet Rebuild of the American Grid

Nobody announced it. There is no programme, no deadline, and no single office in charge. But between the steel mills and the permitting dockets, the largest piece of engineering in the country is being rebuilt one interconnection agreement at a time.

MV

By Marguerite Vale
Edited by Cormac Fyfe · Fact-checked by Ilse Brandt

18 min read Oct 24, 2026 Abilene · ERCOT · Richland
Get stories like this weekly

In this story

    The transformer weighs 380,000 pounds and it is, by any reasonable measure, the least interesting object in this story. It arrived in Abilene on a convoy of eleven trucks at 4 a.m. on a Tuesday in March, took nine hours to set on its pad, and will spend the next forty years doing one thing: stepping 345,000 volts down to 138,000. The interesting part is what had to happen first. The order was placed in 2021. The queue number that mattered was assigned in 2019. The substation it feeds was approved by a county commission that had already been replaced twice.

    The waiting list is the product

    Every conversation I had over the past nine months about the American electricity system eventually arrived at the same place: a spreadsheet. Utilities keep them. Regulators keep them. Project developers keep a copy of a copy, and they all describe roughly the same fact from a different angle — that the binding constraint on new generation in most of the country is no longer money, hardware, or even consent. It is the interconnection queue.

    An interconnection queue is the process by which a generator asks the grid to let it plug in. The operator studies whether the addition will overload anything; if it will, the developer pays for upgrades; then everyone signs something. In theory this takes twelve to eighteen months. In practice, across the regions whose filings I read, the median for a utility-scale solar-plus-storage project now runs between forty-one and fifty-eight months, and roughly seven in ten proposals never reach a signed agreement at all.

    How we reported this

    Marguerite Vale read 214 interconnection agreements, 39 docket filings, and eleven utility integrated resource plans across four regions between January and October 2026, and conducted 42 recorded interviews. Company figures were cross-checked against public filings wherever a private operator made a claim we could not otherwise verify. Three sources spoke on the condition that we not name their employer; we describe their roles precisely instead.

    “People keep asking me when the queue clears,” said Dana Whitlock, who has run interconnection planning for a mid-continent transmission operator since 2018. “The queue isn't a line. It's a market. When you make waiting expensive, some people pay to wait and other people stop asking. It never empties.”

    Who actually buys the steel

    Lead times for large power transformers were, before 2020, a boring number: eight to twelve months for a domestic order, maybe fourteen if you wanted something with unusual taps. Three manufacturers told me the current realistic window for a 345 kV unit is thirty to forty-two months, and that the order book is now written out past 2029.

    The reason is not one war or one tariff. It is that the demand curve moved in two directions at once. Utilities are replacing decades of deferred maintenance at exactly the moment the load forecast stopped being flat. Data-centre interconnection requests in the regions I examined grew by a factor of more than four between 2023 and 2026, and every one of them arrives with a transformer on the same three suppliers' shelves.

    “We are not competing with each other. We are competing with a schedule that everybody wrote in the same year.”

    Procurement director, investor-owned utility, Pacific Northwest

    The boring bottleneck is a kind of steel

    Transformers are wound with grain-oriented electrical steel, a specialist product with fewer producers than there are major economies. Two of the three North American supply relationships I traced funnel through a single rolling mill. When that mill takes an unplanned quarter off — and it did, in 2024, for a maintenance campaign that overran — the delay propagates outward through the whole industry with almost no damping, because nobody holds inventory of a thing that costs what a small house costs.

    A staffing problem wearing a software costume

    Every operator I spoke to has a modernisation programme, and every one of them describes it in the same three words: study automation. The studies themselves are simulations — electromagnetism, load-flow, stability — that were, until recently, run by hand by engineers with names on them.

    Automation helps. It does not, however, change the fact that a study requires a person to sign it. Six of the eleven operators and utilities I interviewed said the same thing in nearly the same terms: they could process twice as many requests with the tools they have, but they cannot find the engineers, because the engineers are 58 and retiring on a schedule that was set before anyone forecast the new load.

    • The study queue is a knowledge queue. Regional models take two years to build and a decade to trust.
    • Standardised contracts have not standardised much. Nearly every state now publishes a form interconnection agreement; the negotiated addenda still take as long as the studies.
    • Repowering is the cheapest kilowatt and the hardest permit. Replacing turbines on an existing wind footprint requires almost no new interconnection study, and almost none of it happens quickly.

    What actually gets built, and where

    Step back from the paperwork and something surprising appears: the buildout is happening anyway. Capacity additions in the regions I examined have exceeded every agency forecast published between 2023 and 2025, year after year, largely because developers learned to work the queue as a portfolio — twelve proposals in, three agreements out, one built.

    That is efficient for a developer and corrosive for a system. The queue is doing, badly and quietly, the job a central plan would otherwise do: deciding which projects get land, which get substations, and which get nothing. The consequence is that the grid that is being rebuilt is not the grid anyone forecast. It is the grid that cleared the paperwork first.

    “We stopped asking whether a project was good. We started asking whether its paperwork was complete.”

    Independent market monitor, Midwest region

    A five-year horizon you can hold in your hand

    In Richland, a substation that has been under construction since 2022 finally energised one feeder in August. I watched the switching order come down by phone, from a county road, at 6:40 p.m. A contractor's foreman told me the crew had been waiting on a single bushing for four months. When it came, they installed it in under two hours.

    Two hours of work, four months of waiting, six years of approvals. That ratio is the honest summary of the American grid rebuild: the physical act is trivially fast and everything around it is slow, and the slowness is not mysterious. It is the aggregate cost of a system that was designed to be marginally adequate and got very good at being careful.

    Whether the next five years look different depends on things that will not appear in a technology section: how many transmission studies a regional operator can staff, whether a mill takes its maintenance campaign on schedule, and whether a county commission in a place with no data centres votes yes on a 230 kV line before the load arrives or after. The transformers are already sold. The wires are already drawn. What remains is the part the country has always found hardest — agreeing, in writing, on who goes first.

    — M.V., filed from Abilene, October 22, 2026
