01 — The premise

One season, one place, and room left in the calendar.

Most itineraries are a list of things not to miss. Ours are a list of things to sit with. A Wanderpost journey is built around a single weather system: the week the maple turns in Higashiyama, the fortnight the wind drops in Torres del Paine, the month the light stays blue past ten in Svolvær.

Then we clear space. Two afternoons with no plan. A long lunch that is the actual itinerary. You will come home able to name the street where you thought best.

02 — Why slow, why us

Four decisions that shape every route we draw.

  1. iWe start from the calendar, not the map

    A season tells you where to go. Cherry, maple, snow-melt, aurora — we plan backwards from the week a place is at its most itself, and we tell you honestly when it is merely busy.

  2. iiOne base, then radiate

    You unpack twice, not eight times. Two nights minimum everywhere, four in the anchor town, so a place can actually accumulate around you instead of flashing past the window.

  3. iiiEight travellers, or nobody

    Small enough for one table, one guide, one conversation. Every departure is privately led, and the guide is the person who wrote the route — not a contractor handed a script.

  4. ivWe leave the blanks in

    Roughly a third of every journey is unscheduled on purpose. That is where the good wrong turns go, and we would not dream of filling them with a coach timetable.

03 — How a journey takes shape

From a first letter to the field notes you send back.

Six to nine months, in five movements. Nothing about it is rushed, including the part where we say no.

  1. Month one

    Your letter

    You tell us the pace you want and the things you will not enjoy — markets at dawn, long drives, cold. We read it before we propose anything, and we write back with a season and two or three places.

    Start an enquiry →

  2. Month two

    A route with holes in it

    You receive a day-by-day draft in a shared document, annotated with why each stay exists and where the blank hours sit. Two rounds of edits are included; most journeys need exactly one.

    Read a sample guide →

  3. Month three

    People before places

    We book the hands, not the hotels: the tea ceremony teacher in Nakagyō, the estancia cook in Patagonia, the third-generation tanner who will actually let you sit and watch. Then the beds around them.

  4. On the ground

    One guide, one telephone number

    Your guide is on the trip from the first evening to the last transfer, along with a local number that answers at two in the morning. Trains, ferries and weather changes are ours to absorb, not yours.

  5. After

    The post comes back

    You send us a paragraph; we add it to the guide with your name on it, and the next traveller plans around what you learned. Sixty-one of our last eighty departures came from that archive.
