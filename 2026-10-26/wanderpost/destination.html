02 — The route we draw

Thirteen days, three bases, following the colour south.

Kyoto turns from the mountains down, roughly ten days earlier in Ōhara than in Higashiyama and a fortnight earlier than Kōyasan. So we start high and finish low. This is the shape of our Deep Autumn journey, with the blank hours marked.

  1. Days 1–2 · Higashiyama

    Land, then walk nowhere

    Nishi-tsuitonbori lane at dusk, when the stone still holds the day’s warmth. Nanzen-ji’s aqueduct on the second morning, before the coaches clear the Kiyomizu car park. No temples scheduled after eleven.

  2. Day 3 · Nakagyō

    Tea, then a long lunch

    A two-hour lesson with Ilse in a machiya that has not been renovated for photographs: whisk, sit, be told the bowl is wrong, try again. Lunch afterwards is the only other item on the day.

  3. Days 4–5 · Ōhara

    Up into the colour

    One hour north by local bus and the temperature drops three degrees. Kamo-jinja’s moss at first light, Sanzen-in’s five-hundred-rakan bronze heads, then the farmstead veranda for the rest of the afternoon with persimmon and nothing else.

  4. Day 6 · Kurama

    Cedar ridge, on foot

    The five-kilometre ridge walk over Kurayama to Kibune, roots and cedars and maples overhead. Cable car down if knees object. Onsen at the bottom, then a train home at dark.

  5. Days 7–8 · Arashiyama

    The west, at 06.45

    With a calligrapher, not a crowd: bamboo grove before the first tour bus, Tenryū-ji garden at opening, Oki temple roof above the river. Afternoon free — we recommend the tram back and a nap.

  6. Day 9 · Free

    Deliberately empty

    Nothing. A bicycle, the Philosopher’s Path, Nishiki market if you want cheese and knives, or the onsen at Kurama for the day. This is the day travellers write about, so we protect it.

  7. Days 10–12 · Kōyasan

    Mountain temples last

    Shinkansen to Shin-Ōsaka, then the winding rack railway up through the cedars. Two nights in a temple lodge: vegetarian dinner, five o’clock bells, Okunoin graves in the blue morning, then the descent.

  8. Day 13 · Out

    Nanki to Kansai

    An hour and a half by limited express to the airport, with time to buy the yatsuhashi you actually like rather than the one in the station.

03 — Where to sleep

Five places we book, and why.

All are small, family-run, and inside walking distance of something worth the morning. We hold rooms at each, which is why we can put you in them the week everyone else is told they are full.

Machiya Gion-nishi

Four rooms in a restored 1901 townhouse, cedar bath, a courtyard garden the size of one tatami. Ten minutes’ walk from Nanzen-ji. Breakfast is rice, miso and an egg you are not consulted about.

£215 a night, twin

Ōhara Farmstead

Eight rooms in a 200-year-old gasshō farmhouse, kerosene lamps in the outer garden, breakfast over a sunken hearth. The maples here turn a full ten days before the city.

£180 a night, including dinner

Kongōsan-in, Kōyasan

A four-hundred-year-old shukubō: futon on tatami, sliding screens onto a rock garden, the best onsen bath on the mountain, and the 05.30 bells you will not regret hearing.

£165 a night, half-board

Hoshiya Ryokan, Arashiyama

On the river, fourteen rooms, three generations. Ask for the upstream side and you will fall asleep to water over stones rather than the footbridge traffic.

£340 a night, twin

Nishijin Loft

Our spare option for extra nights or a solo traveller who wants a kitchen. Plain, quiet, in the old weaving quarter, two minutes from the bus that goes everywhere cheaply.

£96 a night

04 — Eat and drink

Ten things to eat, in the order we eat them.

  • Tamagoyaki at 07.30 from the counter on Miyagawa-dōri — sweet omelette sold out by nine, standing up.
  • Shōjin ryōri, the temple cuisine of Kōyasan: sesame tofu, yuba, burdock. Ask for the second helping; they will bring it.
  • Kaiseki at Kikunoi if the budget stretches — otherwise the lunch version at Hyotei, which is the same chef family and half the price.
  • Matsutake in November, grilled plain, in Ōhara. One night, if the season has been kind.
  • Kamo namban — cold-soba with duck in the evening at Gion Harinavi, a room of ten seats.
  • Obanzai, the Kyoto home-cooking plates: seven small vegetable dishes meant for a farmhouse, not a menu.
  • Yūba and persimmon from the Nishiki market stalls, then the roasted tea at the back of the arcade.
  • Sake from Fushimi, at the brewery taprooms where they pour the unpasteurised draft you cannot buy bottled.
  • Matcha at Jōan, whisked thick, and the sweet bean paste you are supposed to eat first and will not.
  • Kappon in the Pontocho backstreets past eleven — chicken, offal, clear broth, and the local newspaper on the wall.

05 — Getting around

Buy the bus day pass, then walk.

City

Four subway lines and a bus net. A ¥1,100 day bus pass beats the taxi ranks for Higashiyama, and most of what matters sits within 25 minutes on foot of Gion-Shijō. Rent a bicycle for Arashiyama days — ¥800, and the river path is flat.

Out to Ōhara and Kibune

Kokusai bus 17 from the Kyoto Station north platform, just over an hour, ¥750. It is the same bus the day-trippers take, which is why we sleep there instead of visiting it. Trains will not get you into either valley.

Up to Kōyasan

Odakyu limited express from Shimizu-machi, then the cable funicular and a shuttle. Two and a half hours door to temple lodge. Buy the through ticket; the separate fares are a trap for people who cannot count yen at speed.

IC card: get an ICOCA or Suica on arrival at Kansai and never think about single tickets again. It works on every bus, the subway, the private railways and the convenience stores.

06 — Quiet places

Six addresses that are still yours at eight in the morning.

  • Giōan-ji — the small bamboo hut and garden where the writer of Oku no Hosomichi lived. Fifteen minutes, five people, extraordinary light in November.
  • Otagi Nenbutsu-ji — a hillside of maples you climb on a stone stair, and the view down over the city with the crowds still at Kiyomizu.
  • Yōkaichi-guchi — Yakuō-ji’s lower gate before seven, when only the delivery bikes and one monk are moving.
  • The west bank of the Kamo river, north of Nishioji: a gravel path, herons, and rowers who will let you watch for nothing.
  • Rangetsu-in — Myōshin-ji’s sub-temples hold some forty institutions; this one has a thatched hall, a garden of moss, and a two-minute queue on a good day.
  • Ninnaji at the top — the chirimen-dō walkway up through the plantation to the pagoda, and the city spread below it in cold, clear air.

07 — Questions we get asked

Seven honest answers.

Is Kyoto too busy in autumn?

Yes, in the second half of November, at the four famous viewpoints, between ten and four. We plan around that with early openings, the two valleys, and a route that finishes on a mountain. You will share the maples with other people; you will not spend the day in a queue.

Do I need to speak Japanese?

No. Every place we send you to has been briefed on an English-speaking guest, and your guide answers messages. That said, learning “kōnichiwa”, “arigatō gozaimasu” and how to say “no, thank you” changes the texture of a week. We put them on the first page of your field guide.

How much walking is really involved?

Between two and five kilometres a day in the city, mostly flat, with the gravel-and-stone paths of temple grounds. The Kurama ridge is five kilometres with a steady climb. Every walking day has a taxi or cable-car escape, and we build that in rather than mentioning it afterwards.

Can we do Kyoto and Tokyo in one trip?

You can, and many agencies will sell it to you. We do not, because the Shinkansen makes it look cheap in time and expensive in attention: you would spend two days of eleven sitting in a very fast train. Come back in May for the new green and we will plan a second, different city.

What about rain?

Autumn is the driest season here — November averages six wet days, usually short. If it turns, we move you to the Nishiki arcade, a paper workshop, or the onsen at Kurama, all of which are better in the rain than in the sun.

Is a temple stay uncomfortable?

By Western standards it is minimal: futon on tatami, shared bathroom in some lodges, dinner served on the floor at a fixed time, and early bells. It is also the best night’s sleep people report on the whole journey. If you genuinely need a bed, tell us and we will hold a room at the bottom instead.

When should we book?

Six to nine months ahead for autumn. The Ōhara farmstead and Kongōsan-in are the two constraints — five and eight rooms respectively, and our travellers have first claim before we open a departure to the list.
