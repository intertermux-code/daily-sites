Stories

Twelve years of groceries, told by the people who CARRY them.

Every name here gave written permission, chose how they are described, and can ask us to take their story down at any time. Some of these are photographs of volunteers; the neighbors we serve are shown with their own consent, and never at a distribution table.

The long one

The year the tomatoes came back

Food PharmacyClinic referral

Delia Ruiz had been told three times to eat better and given a pamphlet. The fourth time, Dr. Osei wrote her a prescription for vegetables — and sent her to a cannery on Cannery Row.

“I thought it was a joke. A prescription for food.” She picked up twelve weeks of produce, a dietitian visit every six weeks, and a printed list of things she already knew how to cook with. Her A1C came down 1.1 points. Her grocery bill came down too, which is the part nobody in the clinic expected.

Now my grandson eats carrots because I eat carrots. That is the whole story. It is not that complicated when the food is just sitting there.

Delia now volunteers on the Thursday bus, two aisles, always near the tomatoes. She tells new shoppers where the good squash hides. Four hundred and eleven people have enrolled in the Food Pharmacy since she did; about three hundred of them finished twelve weeks, and 71% is the number we are trying to push to 80 by next spring.

Delia RuizFood Pharmacy, year two · Thursday bus volunteer

The numbers behind this story

  • 1,180 produce prescriptions filled
  • −0.8 average A1C at twelve weeks
  • $34 average weekly grocery savings
  • 71% of enrollees finish the program

Follow one family

The Okonjo household, twelve months

Two adults, three kids, one car that needed a transmission in March. Names used with permission; the months are theirs.

  2. MAR
    March — the repair

    $2,400 gone, groceries are next

    The transmission ate the emergency fund. Grace kept the clinic shifts and cut the food budget first, because food is the only line she can shrink in pennies. The kids noticed rice again.

  3. APR
    April — the barbershop

    Felix mentions the two shelves

    Not an intake interview — a haircut conversation. Grace walked to Northgate on a Tuesday, took milk, eggs, carrots and bread, and filled out nothing.

  4. JUN
    June — backpacks start

    School meals stop; the bags do not

    Summer is when children in this valley eat worst. Three bags a week, one treat each, and the middle one quietly includes the peanut butter the youngest will actually eat.

  5. AUG
    August — the kitchen

    Grace signs up for a Wednesday class

    She came for the free dinner and stayed for the roasting tips. She now buys the ugly carrots on purpose. Her A1C, mildly high in spring, moved in the direction her doctor wanted.

  6. OCT
    October — the Thursday bus

    Seven months later, she wears a name tag

    Grace volunteers two aisles on the Eastbank bus, mostly squash, and tells people where the good ones hide. The transmission is fixed. This is not a neat ending — it is a family that stopped choosing between food and a repair.

Our story rules

How we tell these, and how we do not

  • Written permission, kept on file, revocable by one email.
  • Neighbors choose their own name, photo and how far their story goes.
  • No photographs taken at a distribution table, ever.
  • No child’s face used in fundraising without a guardian’s signed form.
  • We publish outcomes we did not like, including the year enrollment fell.
