Twelve years

From one station wagon to a cold chain

Bright Harvest started because Marisol Delgado had a van, a u-haul ramp and a produce wholesaler who threw away grade-two bell peppers.

  2. ’14
    2014

    One station wagon, one church lot

    Four hundred pounds a week, sorted on folding tables behind Trinity Methodist. Twenty-two neighbors on the first Saturday; nine of them came back to help, and they are still here.

  3. ’16
    2016

    The cannery opens its dock

    The port gave us a rent-free bay on Cannery Row. Cold storage, a loading dock that fits a box truck, and finally a place to say yes to a whole pallet instead of a crate.

  4. ’18
    2018

    Route software replaces the whiteboard

    Our first dispatch app let grocers flag surplus with one tap. Rescued volume doubled in seven months, and spoilage on our own floor dropped to under 3%.

  5. ’20
    2020

    Curbside, and 3× the need

    Spring 2020 served 96,000 neighbors a month through a car window. We learned that dignity is mostly logistics: shorter lines, no forms, food you can pick yourself.

  6. ’22
    2022

    Food Pharmacy starts with one clinic

    Dr. Osei wrote the first produce prescription for a patient whose A1C would not move. Two hundred and eighteen enrollments later, the average drop was 0.8 points.

  7. ’24
    2024

    Mobile Market goes to the blocks

    Two retired transit buses refitted with shelving and cold cases. Shoppers choose their own food in a store-shaped space instead of receiving a box they did not pick.

  8. ’26
    Now — 2026

    One million meals a year, and the gap list

    We crossed 1.28 million meals last fiscal year. The plan for this one is protein and dairy, because fresh is solved and shelf-stable does not fix a doctor’s concern.

Mobile Market

Two refitted transit buses become a full-service grocery for an afternoon, parked where the nearest supermarket is a two-bus transfer away.

Neighbors browse, choose and leave with what they will actually eat. There is no eligibility check and no inventory of their household — the only requirement is living in the valley. Buses carry produce, dairy, bakery, eggs and shelf-stable protein, priced at zero and tracked on the same scale as everything else we distribute.

Food Pharmacy

A doctor writes it, we fill it, and the lab work afterwards tells us whether it worked.

Participants enrolled by a clinician at Hale Medical, Riverside Community or one of four partner practices receive twelve weeks of prescribed produce, a dietitian visit every six weeks, and a standing grocery allotment. We report back to the prescriber so the food shows up in the chart the way a prescription would.

Community Kitchens

Forty-five minutes, one pan, and a recipe you can repeat at home on a Tuesday.

Our teaching kitchen sits where the cannery’s boiler room used to be. Neighbors cook the rescue grade — the mis-shaped carrots, the greens that wilt early, the fish near its date — with a chef who grew up on the same block. Classes end with everyone eating together at the same table and a take-home portion for whoever has to get to a night shift.

Kids’ Harvest

Friday afternoon, eleven schools, and a backpack heavy enough to get a child to Monday.

When school meals stop for the summer, so does a lot of children’s food. We pack weekend bags with shelf-stable breakfast, fresh fruit that survives three days, and one thing that is purely a treat — because childhood is not a nutrition study. Teachers and counselors request bags discreetly, and never have to explain who asked.

The Partner Site Network

We are a warehouse; the sites are the front door. Thirty-four of them, run by people who already know their block.

A barbershop, a laundromat, two clinics, a mosque, a fire station, nine school lots, a senior tower lobby and the rest run by churches and community groups. Each site gets a refrigerated delivery, shelving we install, food safety training for its volunteers, and the rule that matters: the neighbor chooses, nobody fills out a form, and nobody is photographed.

Recovery Tech Lab

The dispatch app we built is free for any food bank in the country. Nine already run it.

Surplus flagging, cold-chain temperature logging, weigh-scale reporting and the gap dashboard that tells our buyers what to purchase on Monday morning. Everything is open source, everything is documented, and we spend two staff days a month helping other banks install it — because a rescued pallet in another county is still a rescued pallet.
