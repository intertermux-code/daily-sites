The Last Mile Is a Fiber Run

Three towns in eastern Oregon spent four years and $11.4 million building the network their incumbents would not. What they found at the bottom of the trench became a template the boardrooms refused to copy — and the reason a county of 77,000 people now sells dark strand to a company that once called them unprofitable.

By Elena Sokolova

Infrastructure Correspondent · Reporting from Umatilla County

Pendleton, Ore. — The excavator belonged to a water district, which is the kind of detail that decides whether a fiber project happens. On a Tuesday in March 2024, Dawn Ferris stood at the edge of a trench on Southwest Third Street and watched a man she had known since high school cut eight inches of asphalt for a strand of glass that would not carry a single byte for another eleven months.

Pendleton, Umatilla and Milton-Freewater share a watershed, a school bus route and, until this spring, essentially nothing else. What they did not share was a fiber path. The three towns sit forty minutes from the nearest major internet interchange, which is roughly the distance a carrier will drive to reach a customer and slightly farther than it will drive to reach a promise.

In February the county finished a network that is 61.4 route-miles long, serves 4,118 households, and cost $11.4 million to build — about $2,768 per passing, which is a number I checked four times because it is the entire argument. In the same period, the incumbent that serves this county told investors that rural Oregon was “structurally unaddressable.”

The grant with a condition

In the winter of 2022, Umatilla County received a state broadband grant with a clause that reads, in the final paragraph of a nineteen-page agreement, that the money must be spent jointly by at least two jurisdictions or returned. It was not designed to encourage cooperation. It was designed to make non-cooperation expensive.

Pendleton's public works director, Nadia Okoro, had spent nine years asking incumbents for a build she was told the census blocks did not justify. She had the letters. She kept them in a bankers box under her desk, and she brought the box to the first joint meeting, which is the kind of gesture that ends a debate faster than a slide deck.

“We weren't trying to become an ISP,” she told me. “We were trying to become a place where the ISP stopped lying to us.”

The three cities agreed to a structure that would have made a consultant nervous: a joint powers authority with a governing board of seven, a two-person staff, and one hired civil engineer. That engineer, Ravi Chandrasekaran, had designed storm systems for eleven years and had never laid a strand of glass. He now runs a network with 99.996 percent availability, a figure he recalculates weekly because he does not trust a vendor's arithmetic more than his own.

What a trench actually costs

The folklore of broadband construction holds that fiber is expensive. It is not. A mile of single-mode strand costs roughly what a used car costs. Conduit, the plastic pipe the glass lives in, is a few dollars per foot at the counter.

What is expensive is the act of putting it underground in a place where somebody already owns the surface. Umatilla County's budget breaks down as follows:

  • 61.4 route-miles of new conduit: $4.9 million, or 43 percent of the total. Almost all of it was pavement restoration.
  • 412 crossings — rail, irrigation canal, highway, two natural-gas lines — $2.3 million, 20 percent of the total for things that are each shorter than a city bus.
  • Splicing, electronics and the central office: $2.6 million, 23 percent.
  • Design, permitting, legal and the eight years of prior correspondence: $1.6 million, 14 percent.

Read that list again with the industry's framing in mind. Roughly one dollar in five went into the holes other people's utilities had already made, and the largest single line is a trench that a water district owned the equipment to dig. Fiber is cheap. Permission is not.

“Every dollar we spent was a fight with somebody's schedule. The glass was the easy part. The glass never once asked for a fee.”

The crossing problem

A crossing is where your conduit must go under something that is already under the ground. In rural Oregon the “something” is frequently a Union Pacific main line, and the negotiation for a single bore under that line took Chandrasekaran fourteen months and produced a document set heavier than the equipment.

The county's answer was not to negotiate better. It was to stop needing to. Whenever the trench opened, the crew laid spare conduit — thirty-one percent more pipe than the network required, at a marginal cost of $61,000. That decision, made in a two-line budget amendment in 2023, is now the financial basis of the whole project.

How we reported this story

Elena Sokolova spent nine days in Umatilla County in September 2026, reviewed 214 pages of joint-powers board minutes, three bid tabulations and the county's audited construction ledger, and interviewed 17 people including two former incumbent field managers who spoke on the record. Every figure cited is cross-checked against a document; where the county's own numbers conflict, we say so in the text and footnote the difference.

Twelve thousand feet of spare conduit

In April, the county began leasing empty pipe to anyone who wanted it. A cellular carrier needed backhaul to three towers on the I-84 frontage. A food-processing plant in Milton-Freewater wanted a redundant path after a backhoe cut its only fiber in 2025 and took its line offline for nine hours. A university extension wanted a research link.

The county now books $317,000 a year in dark-strand and conduit revenue from eleven customers, against a debt service of $498,000. The shortfall is covered by retail subscriptions to 2,884 households paying $52 a month for symmetric gigabit — a price that is $21 below what the incumbent charged for a nominally equivalent product in the same postal codes.

The economics are not magic, and they are not a secret. They are the result of an entity that was allowed to spend its own money on its own dirt, and that kept 31 percent of the trench it dug for other people.

What the incumbents saw

I asked four carriers, in writing, whether the Umatilla model was replicable. Two did not answer. One told me that municipal overbuild “distorts the capital signal in exactly the places private network investment is most fragile.” The fourth, whose field manager had toured the county's central office in July, said only that its own rural build program had been “reprioritized.”

The word that recurs in the industry's own filings is churn. A municipal network does not chase a subscriber; it holds one. Umatilla County's retail churn rate is 0.6 percent monthly, against a national consumer-broadband average near 2.1 percent. When Chandrasekaran published that comparison in a slide, an analyst from a mid-cap carrier emailed him to ask whether the methodology was real, and then, when he sent it, asked nothing further.

There is a version of this story where the county wins and the industry loses. The version that actually happened is narrower and more annoying: a small public body proved that the constraint was never the glass, and every company that had used the glass as an explanation now has to explain something else.

A promise you keep weekly

On a Wednesday evening in the central office — a former pump station, 1,900 square feet, one air handler — the county publishes its own outage history as a plain HTML table. It has done this every Monday since service began, including the two weeks in January when the numbers were bad and nobody was obliged to look at them.

That table is the least photogenic thing in this story and, I would argue, the most consequential. A network is not a cable. A network is a promise that somebody will answer when the cable fails, and the evidence of whether they will is usually buried in a support queue in another time zone.

Okoro keeps the bankers box under her desk still. She told me she intends to donate it to the county archive once the last incumbent representative who cited “geography” in a public filing retires.
