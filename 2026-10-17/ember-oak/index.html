02 Why we build a fire every morning at 7:15

A restaurant that starts with wood and ends with a table you do not want to leave.

Tomas Breda opened Ember & Oak in a former kiln warehouse with one rule: if it cannot be cooked over live coals, it does not belong on the card.

We cook the way kitchens used to have to.

Split oak goes in cold. An hour later it is a bed of coals at three temperature zones — searing at the back, roasting in the middle, and a cool shelf at the front where fish and vegetables finish slowly in their own smoke.

There is no pilot light here, no combi oven, no sous-vide bag. What you taste is wood, salt, time, and whatever the Willamette Valley gave us that week. Some nights that is a dry-aged ribeye. Some nights it is a carrot, buried in embers for forty minutes and split open with cultured butter.

We started with twelve seats and a secondhand hearth. The warehouse kept the kiln marks on its walls, so we left them. Ten years on, the room still smells like a forest after rain and a butcher shop after closing, and we have not found a better description of what we are aiming for.

04 One service, start to finish

Ten hours between a cold grate and your first course.

  1. 01

    Split the wood

    Oak and almond, seasoned eighteen months in the yard behind the building. Two staff spend forty minutes splitting and stacking before anyone strikes a match.

  2. 02

    Light cold

    Nothing but kindling and a match. No lighter fluid, no gel — both leave a taste on a halibut collar, and we learned that the expensive way.

  3. 03

    Build the zones

    By ten the coals are raked into three beds: searing at the back, roasting in the middle, a cool smoke shelf at the front for fish, leaves and anything you want to think about.

  4. 04

    Taste, then write

    At two the kitchen eats what the fire is giving that day. The card is written after that meal, not before it — which is why the 6:30 seating sometimes gets a surprise.

  5. 05

    Bank it out

    Service ends, the remaining coals are banked and covered. Wednesday’s fire is often still alive when we light Thursday’s.

The counter is the best seat, and it is the hardest to get.

Twelve stools face the hearth directly. You will be told what you are eating before it lands, you will be handed it by the person who cooked it, and on a Friday you will be asked whether you want one more spoon of the pan juices.

We hold six of the twelve for walk-ins from 5:00 pm and release the rest to reservations at 10:00 am on the day. If the counter is full, the main room is quieter, the same fire is twenty feet away, and the food is identical.

06 Behind the fire

The four people you will actually meet.

Nine in the kitchen, seven in the room. Everybody cooks a night a month on the pass, including the owner.

Tomas Breda

Chef-Owner

Fourteen years in New Orleans and Copenhagen kitchens before the kiln warehouse. Still lights Wednesday’s fire himself.

Astrid Lund

Executive Sous Chef · Fire

Runs the coals and the dry-age room. Wrote the ash-baking method that the carrot dish is built on.

Marguerite Vale

Pastry

Bakes entirely in the hearth’s residual heat — nothing in her repertoire needs an oven, so her station has none.

Gerald Mott

Pitmaster & Cellar

Splits the wood, buys the wine, and has not been wrong about a pairing on the counter yet.
