File 001 · Foundry No. 4

Three Engineers,
One Dead Machine
Room.

Copperline exists because a brass foundry went bust in 2008 and left its tools hanging on the hooks. We did not have to install much. We had to learn what the cranes were for.

2009 · The purchase

We Bought It For The Windows

Ilse Vandermeer was drawing coastal defence structures; Marguerite Okonjo ran a water-treatment plant; Peter Ashe kept the drawings. The auction catalogue listed Foundry No. 4 as “industrial shell, contents to clear, 21 south-facing factory windows.”

They bought it for £118,000 with a loan against two houses and a shared car, on the theory that a room built to hold 40 men and four furnaces would comfortably hold a brewhouse and twice that many drinkers. The first weekend they swept eight tonnes of brass swarf out of the basement. The second weekend Peter found the nameplate: COPPERLINE & SONS, FOUNDERS, 1911, still bolted above the main switch.

We took the name because the pipework was already there — 900 metres of it, some of it still holding water from the last shift in 2008. We cleaned it, tested it, and put glycol through the good runs. Every cold line in this building is older than the beer in it.

The room, measured

  • 1,140 m² under the roof, 8.2 m to the crane rail
  • 21 factory windows, all original wire-glass
  • One 1911 overhead crane, still rated to 3 tonnes
  • Brick walls 508 mm thick — the cellar never needed air conditioning
  • One tap from a 1936 fire main, still used for the hose reel

How It Actually Went

2009

Sold For Cash, Swept For Two Months

Foundry No. 4 bought at auction. Eight tonnes of brass swarf out of the basement, 21 windows glazed back in by a retired window-maker named Tom Bellhouse who took payment in beer rights. Those rights are still honoured.

2010

The Copper Decision

A new stainless cold line was cheaper than testing the old pipework. We tested it instead: 71% sound. The 287 metres that passed now carry glycol, and the brewhouse we ordered was jacketed in copper to match the nameplate.

2011

Refused, Then Allowed

Planning refused the change of use over noise. We built a second wall in front of the patio doors, appealed, and got condition 9 discharged eleven months later. The 10 hL brewhouse arrived in crates in December and sat in the hall until the wiring was signed off.

2012

Batch No. 001, Gone In One Evening

Twelve gallons of amber ale, brewed for the crew who lifted the brewhouse into place. Licensed 44-B-1911, doors open 14 April. Firebox Amber became No. 07 the year we ran out of single digits, and it has never left the list.

2015

Two Hundred Litres Of Wild Beer, On Purpose

A culture took hold in the west wall brick — we found it because a saison we meant to dump had gone beautifully tart. We isolated it as WC-3, and it now drives the barrel programme. No other brewery will get it out of us.

2018

Crane Rail To Cask Room

We hung the cask conditioning room from the original 3-tonne crane rail rather than cutting new steel. Eleven casks rest under the hall at 12°C, pulled by hand through four towers at the bar.

2019

Rivet & Rye Puts Us On A National List

No. 12 took a silver at the regional showcase and sold out of cans in nine days. We responded the only way we know: bought a second can seamer, and doubled the rye order.

2021

Shut, Re-Shelved, Reopened

Eleven months of no room and a windowless bottle shop. We canned everything that would take it, sold 4,100 packs, and kept every member of staff on payroll. The patio awning went up that summer, welded on the old crane hook.

2023

Grain Contract With Hallow Road

A three-year agreement: every kilo of spent grain and every drum of hop waste to Femi Adeyemi's farm, six miles out, in exchange for barley grown on the same land twice. Our truck turns around empty on purpose.

2026

Batch No. 621, Seamer No. 2 In Place

Seventeen numbers on the wall, eighteen taps, 94,000 gallons a year, and the same three founders on the line most weekends. The newest hire is a 19-year-old apprentice who sweeps the roaster and will not stop asking about the porter.

On the line · 2026

Nine People, Fourteen Jobs

Nobody here has a marketing title. Between them they mill, mash, can, cellard, pour, fix and cook.

Founder · Brewer

Marguerite Okonjo

Ran municipal water treatment for eleven years before Copperline. She treats every batch of liquor by the same charts she used for the reservoir, and she still controls the mash temperatures personally because the first five batches were wrong and she has not forgotten.

Brews: No. 07, No. 12, No. 31, the barrel blends

Founder · Plant

Ilse Vandermeer

Structural engineer. She drew the cask room into the crane rail, sized the glycol loop off the surviving copper, and rebuilt the can seamer twice from parts ordered against a 1974 manual. Her desk is a switch cabinet with a lamp on it.

Keeps: the pipework drawings, every valve log since 2012

Founder · Cellar

Peter Ashe

Draught technician before he was anything else. Runs the cellar, the hand pumps and the keg returns, and he numbers the chalk marks on the wall by hand. If a line tastes wrong on a Friday, it was Peter who found it before you did.

Owns: 18 tap lines, four cask towers, one very old hose

Bar & Kitchen · Head

Danneke Roos

Started on the bar in 2014, runs service now and writes the food list against whatever is conditioning. She has a standing rule that nothing leaves the pass without a pint suggestion attached, and she is right often enough that we print it on the board.

Pairs: No. 55 with cured fish, No. 42 with venison

House rules

Five Rules We Do Not Bend

They cost us money every year and they are the only reason the beer tastes the same twice.

Rule 01

One Number, One Beer

A recipe keeps its number forever and we never brew two at once. If you see No. 07 on the wall it is the same card it was in 2012, with the same grain bill and one line of amendments at the bottom.

Rule 02

Nothing Leaves The Building Warm

Kegs, cans and bottles are cold-chain from the filler to your glass. We turn deliveries down. Last year we refused three loads and one very understanding haulier.

Rule 03

Print The Whole Grain Bill

Every card shows malt, hops, yeast and batch size. No hidden syrup, no “brewed with”, no extract pretending to be all-malt, no vague word like “specialty” without a percentage.
