間

Our only design rule

Leave room between the things you do

Japanese gardens call the meaningful emptiness ma — the pause that gives the notes around it shape. Most itineraries have no ma at all.

A day with three sights is a day of queues, taxi queues, and a hotel bar you are too wired to enjoy. A day with one sight, and a long bench between, is how you end up describing the light instead of the list.

We build to a ceiling, not a floor: no more than two commitments before lunch, and never a travel day straight onto a walk. If a plan you like breaks that rule, we will tell you plainly, and we will offer to cut something.

  • Arrival day is yours. We book the transfer, then leave. Jet-lag is part of the route.
  • Two unscheduled days per fortnight. Ask us what travellers did with theirs — the answers are the best thing on our website.
  • One long stay beats three short ones. A week in one address costs less than three nights each in three, and lands twice as deep.

How a journey gets made

Three letters, then a route book

手紙

Step one · week one

You write, we listen

Send the enquiry form or a voice note — whichever you think in. We reply within two working days with questions, not a brochure: what you actually want to be alone with, and what you would hate to be rushed through.

Step two · week three

A draft, in pencil

You receive a marked-up route: stays, transfers, the two empty days, and a candid note about what we would change. Nothing is booked and nothing is charged until you have cut, moved, or swapped at least one thing.

Step three · departure minus ten

The letterpress book

Printed, bound, couriered to your door: annotated maps, phrases that actually get used, opening hours nobody publishes, and a phone number that reaches a human. The digital copy comes too, for the taxi.
