§ 00 — By Way of Introduction

Seventeen Years on Alma Lane

A boot-repair shop, a flood, one very stubborn rule about stock, and four people who can tell you what almost any book in this building is for. That is the whole story, and it is longer than we expected.

“We did not set out to open a bookshop. We set out to keep the books we wanted to read in one room, and the room got a door and a till.” — Iris Halloran, founder, from the 2009 lease.

§ 01

How the Shop Came About

In the spring of 2009, Iris Halloran had spent nine years as a buyer for a chain that was already beginning to disappear, and had formed the opinion that a shop which could not explain its own stock was only a warehouse with a till. She took the lease on number fourteen because it was cheap, damp, and had eleven shelves already built into the walls by somebody who had repaired boots for a living.

The first month sold forty-one books. The second sold sixty. Word arrived the way it does in a neighbourhood: somebody’s aunt wanted a specific translation of Odyssey, and the shop found it in five days; somebody else’s son would only read books with dragons in and left with The Phantom Tollbooth and has since asked for Le Guin. The list of things we can source became the reason people came in from the rain.

By 2011 the shop had a bell on the door, a card terminal bought secondhand, and a paper club book that Marguerite started because four regulars kept asking one another to read the same novel. It ran to nine names before the month was out. We still keep the original — it lives in a case by the till, and it is the only object in this building we would not sell at any price.

In November 2017 a pipe gave way above the basement and took three thousand books. The neighbourhood restocked us: donations arrived by the box for eight weeks, and fourteen of them are still on the downstairs shelf with a small pencilled note inside the front cover naming who sent them. That is when we started writing shelf cards for everything we recommended, which is now the part of the job visitors notice first.

Today we are four full-time booksellers, a part-time children’s buyer, one binder on Tuesdays, and roughly nine hundred club members. The floor still creaks in the same four places. We have kept all of this deliberately small: eleven thousand titles is about as many as four people can honestly explain.

The pencilled notes are in four handwritings. We have never once been asked to remove one. Marginalia — 2017, basement

§ 02

The Shorter Version, Dated

Eleven entries from a seventeen-year ledger, chosen because each one changed how the shop runs.

  1. 2009

    Eleven shelves and a borrowed terminal

    The shop opens at fourteen Alma Lane on the fourteenth of April. Forty-one books sold in month one; the kettle worked from day one.

  2. 2011

    The club book begins

    Nine names, one novel, one Thursday. Marguerite keeps the list by hand; the bell on the door is fitted the same autumn.

  3. 2012

    The rule goes on the wall

    “Nothing gets stocked because it is easy to buy” pinned above the till after a distributor offer we turned down twice.

  4. 2014

    Ines starts binding

    A secondhand bench arrives from a closed print shop. We now repair and re-case our own stock, and run the binding workshop from it.

  5. 2015

    Upstairs becomes a room

    The loft is boarded and the long table built from our own old shelving. Twenty-four seats; the first lecture sells out in a week.

  6. 2017

    The flood

    Three thousand books lost in November. The neighbourhood sends four boxes a week for two months; the shop re-shelves itself by January.

  7. 2019

    Rare & Secondhand Fair

    Nine dealers in the building for the first time. It becomes the busiest Friday of our year, and stays free to enter.

  8. 2020

    The counter on the pavement

    Ten months of door collection and a bike courier named Sol. Two hundred households took their first book club from a shop they could not enter.

  9. 2023

    A lift to the long table

    The second floor finally stops being a staircase. The aisles are re-spaced to push a wheelchair the full length of both floors.

  10. 2025

    Eleven thousand titles

    The count goes up on the window by hand, in gold leaf, by a signwriter from the next street who had the shop’s first book.

  11. 2026

    Still four people, still open till ten

    Four clubs, fourteen events in November, and no plan whatever to become a chain.

§ 04

What We Hold To

Written up in 2012 after an argument, revised twice since. Not a mission statement — closer to a set of house rules for the trade.

  • Nothing is stocked because it is easy to buyi.
  • Every recommendation gets written downii.
  • Better texts before cheaper onesiii.
  • A shop is not an audition — nobody’s reading is gradediv.
  • Free events stay free; paid ones say what the money buysv.
  • We will tell you when a book is too expensive for what you wantvi.
  • Neighbourhood first: the bakery, the school, the libraryvii.

“A good shop is a set of arguments you can walk around in. Ours happen to be shippable and smell faintly of glue.”

Iris HalloranFounder, at the counter
Rule (iv) was added after a man apologised for preferring Agatha Christie to Dostoevsky. He now comes to Poetry Late. Marginalia — house rules, p. 1

Access & comfort

Level entrance on Alma Lane; aisles wide enough for a wheelchair end to end; lift to the upstairs room; induction loop in the back room; one accessible toilet on the ground floor. Assistance dogs always welcome, and Marguerite keeps biscuits for them behind the till.

Monday Quiet Hour, 7–8 p.m.: no music, no tannoy, lights down. The best hour in the shop for anyone finding a busy room hard.
