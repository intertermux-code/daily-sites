The Maintainers Holding Up the Modern Internet

Four hundred people keep the world’s software running. Most of them have never been paid for it. Signal spent five months with twenty-seven of them, in eleven countries, and found that the real single point of failure in your stack is not a codebase — it is somebody’s calendar.

Reporting and text by Dana Whitfield
Open Source Desk · Filed 22 Oct 2026, 06:00 CET

18 min · 41,902 reads · 6 corrections logged

The message arrived at 03:47 and, like most messages to an unpaid maintainer, it was wrong. A twenty-two-year-old in Rotterdam had discovered what he believed was a critical parsing flaw in a small library that reads date strings. He was correct about the flaw. He was incorrect about the library, which he had confused with a fork that stopped receiving updates in 2021, and incorrect about the severity, and incorrect about who he was writing to.

Peter Hallberg answered him anyway. That is the pattern. It is the pattern in Malmö and in Kyoto and in a farmhouse outside McCook, Nebraska with two broadband lines and a rack in the boiler room. It is the pattern that holds up, with remarkable exactness, the software your bank runs on.

Hallberg, 54, maintains the date library. He has done it for eleven years. In that time he has reviewed 4,213 pull requests, shipped 388 releases, and been paid a total of $1,400 — once, by a foundation that wanted a specific bug fixed before a conference. He does not consider this unusual. When we sent him the dependency graph showing how many packages his code sits underneath, a number in the low tens of thousands, he read it and said: “That is the wrong way to count. You should count the people who did not have to write a parser.”

What the numbers actually say

To find out how much of the world’s software rests on how few hands, we did not use a survey. Surveys in this ecosystem have a well-documented problem: the people with no time answer last, and the people with a public relations department answer first.

Instead we built a ledger. With help from our data desk, we took the 1.2 million most-downloaded packages across npm, PyPI, crates.io, Packagist and Maven Central, and for each of the top 3,000 by transitive dependency count we recorded who held write access, whether that person had a disclosed employer, and how many commits in the last twelve months came from someone other than the top author. Where the answer was “one person, no employer, all commits,” we flagged it as a single-owner package.

Of the 3,000 packages we examined, 812 are single-owner. Of those 812, we were able to confirm a paying employer for the maintainer in 199 cases. That leaves 613 packages — including 41 that sit directly underneath the build tooling of at least three national payment systems — maintained by somebody who, as far as any public record shows, does this for free.

None of that is a secret. It has been said, correctly, for years. What was new to us was the second table.

“You are asking me when I last had a holiday. I am going to answer with a release number, because that is the same thing.” — Anaïs Ferreira, maintainer of a TLS utility used by four European tax agencies

The second table: time, not money

We asked each maintainer we interviewed to reconstruct one ordinary week. Not their worst week — we specifically did not want the incident, because the incident gets a keynote. We wanted Tuesday. Twenty-two of twenty-seven produced something like a list. We put them side by side.

  • Median hours per week spent on the project: 11.5. Median hours paid: 0.
  • Median number of contexts switched per day — that is, how many times the person left the project to do something that pays rent: four.
  • Maintainers who described a formal handover plan for their project: two of twenty-seven.
  • Maintainers who had been contacted by a security researcher in the last year about a bug that was real: nine, of whom three said the disclosure window offered to them was shorter than the time they needed to read the report.

The number that stayed with us, though, is this: eleven of the twenty-seven said their single biggest source of dread was not a bug, not a attack, and not a fork. It was a sentence. “Could you take a look at this PR?” They were not afraid of the work. They were afraid of the backlog of other people’s reasonable requests accumulating while they were at a job, or asleep, or at their child’s school meeting.

A short history of the promise that made this

The reason there are so few maintainers and so many packages is not negligence. It was a design decision made in good faith, and it worked.

Between 1998 and 2004, the shared assumption in the free and open source world was that software was a gift and that a gift did not need a budget line. The practical effect was astonishing: a generation of engineers built infrastructure that nobody had to negotiate a licence for. When npm launched in January 2010 with a package manager that treated every module as independently publishable, it was not inventing a culture. It was industrialising one that already existed.

What the industry then did, at every level from the solo developer to the Fortune 100, was consume that gift at scale and never send an acknowledgement. Not out of malice. There was no mechanism. A dependency tree does not display a person.

Funding arrived late and arrived as a grant economy. We asked all twenty-seven maintainers whether they had applied for money from any of the well-known open source funds in the past three years. Fourteen had. Two had received it. The average length of the application form, according to the ones we obtained, is 41 minutes; the average grant, $18,000; the average reporting requirement, three pages of prose and a spreadsheet, annually, from people whose median available hours per week is 11.5.

The company that solved it internally, and quietly

Not all of it is bleak, and one counter-example is worth describing in detail because it is so rarely copied.

Ines Nunes runs platform engineering at a mid-sized Portuguese insurer with 1,900 employees and no developer-relations budget. In 2023 she put a line in the department’s operating plan: for every open source package that appeared in more than forty of the company’s internal services, the company would pay the maintainer a retainer. Not a donation. A retainer, with a scope document, a named contact on Nunes’s side, and an expectation of response-time SLAs that the maintainer was free to negotiate down.

They now have eleven retainers. The largest is €42,000 a year; the smallest is €6,000. Two of the maintainers are the same people who told us, that month, that they had been considering archiving their projects. Nunes has never announced this. When we asked why, she said: “The moment you make dependency funding a press release, the maintainers become a story about us. They do not need that. They need the invoice to be a normal cost centre.”

The failure nobody planned

In March, one of the 613 broke. Not dramatically. The maintainer, a 39-year-old systems administrator in Ontario, took four weeks of unpaid leave for a family illness and did not say anything on any of the channels where his project lived, because he did not think four weeks counted as an absence worth announcing.

During those four weeks, a CVE was published against his code. It turned out to be low severity and, later, not entirely his package’s fault. But the response latency — nineteen days to a first substantive reply — produced a cascade: two downstream maintainers forked, one distribution patched independently, a vulnerability scanner vendor raised his project’s risk score, and three companies filed internal tickets describing him, in the language of a risk register, as “an unmaintained external dependency with single-person bus factor.”

He came back to 214 notifications and a fork with more GitHub stars than the original. He has not archived it, but he has stopped merging. When we spoke, four months later, he was explicit that the incident had not made him angry at anyone in particular. He was tired in a way that had a shape: he now knew the cost of taking leave from something he had never been paid for, and the knowledge itself had become a maintenance burden.

“Nobody attacked my project. I just had a life event, and the internet treated that as a vulnerability class.” — Systems administrator, Ontario, on the March incident

What would actually change it

We put this question to all twenty-seven and got, in substance, five answers. They are cheap, and none of them is the one the industry keeps proposing.

  1. Paid leave from the project, not for the project. Four maintainers raised the same idea unprompted: the useful intervention is a fund that pays a maintainer’s ordinary costs while someone else holds the repo for six weeks. Not a research grant. A holiday.
  2. Make the dependency tree show a human. Two tooling groups have built prototypes that display the number of distinct committers behind a package before you install it. Every install UX we tested hid this by default.
  3. Retainers over grants. Nunes’s model, and the reason is administrative: a retainer has a renewal date, and renewal forces a conversation. Grants end and nothing has to be reported to anyone.
  4. Disclosure windows that respect a day job. Nine maintainers asked for the same change to coordinated disclosure policy — a default 90-day window measured from the date of acknowledgement, not the date of the report, with an explicit carve-out for unpaid maintainers.
  5. Stop calling it a commons. A commons has rules and a governance body. What we found is closer to a public road maintained by whoever lives next to the gate.

The end of the week

Hallberg did fix the flaw the Rotterdam student described, in the wrong library, three days after we last spoke to him. He shipped the release at 05:40 on a Saturday because the version before had a deprecation warning that would have broken a build at a company he cannot name, and because, as he put it, “if I leave the warning, somebody spends their Sunday on it, and there are enough Sundays.”

He is not an idealist. He said, more than once, that he resents the arrangement and intends to keep working inside it for as long as the shipyard schedules allow. When we asked what he would say to a company reading this and thinking about their own risk register, he was quiet for a while and then gave an answer that we have thought about every day since: “Tell your engineers that if they fix something upstream, it is work. Billable work. Do not put it in a nice paragraph about community. Put it in the sprint.”

Method, sources and corrections

  1. Dependency counts taken from public registry metadata on 14 August 2026, cross-checked against OpenSSF criticality scores; the ledger, the 3,000-package list and our extraction script are published at signal.press/data/maintainers-2026.
  2. Weekly reconstructions were conducted by scheduled call or written interview between 2 June and 30 September 2026. Twenty-two of twenty-seven participants reviewed their own quoted figures before publication; five declined review and are cited in aggregate only.
  3. Grant figures are drawn from the published reports of five open source funds covering 2023–2025; the 41-minute application estimate comes from timed completions of the forms by two of our reporters.
  4. The March incident is described with the maintainer’s consent and without his name, in line with our policy on identifying individuals exposed to harassment. We have read the CVE, the advisory timeline, and the fork’s issue tracker; the nineteen-day figure is the gap between public disclosure and first substantive reply in the project’s issue tracker.
  5. Ines Nunes and her employer were interviewed on the record. The retainer amounts are documented in purchase orders she supplied and which we verified with one of the eleven maintainers.
  6. Correction (22 Oct): an earlier version of this article gave the median weekly hours as 9.5. The correct figure is 11.5; we miscounted respondents who reported time in fifteen-minute blocks.
