The Quiet Coup: How three maintainers rewrote the rules of software.

They never pitched a deck. They never raised a round. For seven years, three engineers in three different time zones have been steering a piece of infrastructure that now underwrites 18% of all public internet traffic — and the industry only noticed when they threatened to stop.

By Elara Voss · Senior Correspondent · Published September 28, 2026 · 23 min read · Reykjavík / Taipei / Porto Alegre

The meeting lasted eleven minutes and twenty-two seconds. Three people were in the room. Two were dialing in from hotel Wi-Fi — one from a Marriott outside Taipei, the other from a borrowed office above a bakery in Porto Alegre. The third was sitting in her kitchen in Reykjavík, watching steam rise off a cup she had forgotten to drink.

There was no agenda. There were no slides. The only artifact on the table was a single page, printed on A4, with four bullet points. The first bullet read: We can stop maintaining the library on January 1. The second read: We will stop maintaining the library on January 1 unless the conditions below are met. The third and fourth bullets were the conditions.

At the time, none of them had a title you would recognize. None of them worked for a company whose logo would be familiar to a reader of this publication. They were, in the industry's formal vocabulary, maintainers — a word that sounds bureaucratic and is in fact the most powerful verb in modern software.

1. The invisible middle

Every piece of software you have used in the last seventy-two hours depends on a library you have never heard of, maintained by a person you will never meet, on a schedule no one is paying them to keep. This is not an exaggeration and it is not a metaphor. It is, as of 2026, the empirical structure of the public internet.

A 2024 audit by the Linux Foundation counted 11.2 million public packages across the major registries. A follow-up study this spring, conducted by a team at ETH Zurich, found that 62% of those packages depend — directly or transitively — on fewer than four thousand human beings. Four thousand people. In a global industry of roughly 28 million professional developers, that is a concentration of dependency that would, in any other domain, require a regulatory framework. In software, it requires only a GitHub notification.

“We were never asked to run the internet. We just kept saying yes to one more pull request.”

— Hrafnhildur Jónsdóttir, Reykjavík

The three maintainers I spoke with for this piece — Hrafnhildur Jónsdóttir in Iceland, Lín Yǔchéng in Taiwan, and Rafaela Souza in Brazil — are not colleagues in any formal sense. They do not share an employer, a Slack, or a calendar. They met for the first time in person in March, at a conference in Berlin that none of them had been paid to attend. They had, however, been reading each other's commit messages for almost a decade.

2. The library

The library in question — which we are calling lib-chronos at the request of its maintainers, who are not seeking publicity — is a small piece of software that does one thing: it converts timestamps between time zones, accounting for the full historical record of daylight saving transitions, leap seconds, and the occasional political redrawing of a time zone boundary.

It is, by lines of code, unremarkable. The current release is 412 lines. It has a single dependency. Its test suite, which runs in under a second, contains 1,847 cases. Its documentation, last updated in April, is 920 words.

And yet: as of September 2026, an independent analysis of package download graphs placed lib-chronos — either directly or as a transitive dependency — inside the software supply of 1.2 billion active devices. It is in your phone's calendar. It is in the backend of the airline reservation system you used last summer. It is in the firmware of the MRI machine in a hospital you have not yet had to visit. It is in the point-of-sale terminal at the corner bakery.

None of this was planned. The library was written in 2011 by a graduate student in Uppsala as a side project to fix a bug he had noticed in a dating app he was using. He released it under the MIT license, went to work at a bank, and stopped thinking about it. The student is now a senior engineer at a fintech firm and declined to be interviewed for this piece. The library, however, did not stop.

3. The handoff

Open source libraries do not, as a rule, die. They are inherited. Someone files an issue. Someone else opens a pull request. Eventually the original author stops responding, and the responder becomes, by default and without ceremony, the maintainer. There is no HR department to onboard them. There is no offer letter. There is only the repository, and the inbox that now belongs to them.

Hrafnhildur became the maintainer of lib-chronos in 2019. She was 26, working as a junior backend engineer at a logistics startup, and had been contributing to the library for about a year — mostly fixing small bugs she had encountered at work. When the original maintainer stopped responding to emails, the GitHub notification inbox — which had, until then, been a minor nuisance — became, overnight, a job.

“I thought it would be a few hours a week,” she told me, over coffee in a Reykjavík café that smelled faintly of wool and wet asphalt. “It was, at the time, maybe four or five issues a month. Then there were twenty. Then there were a hundred. Then there were two hundred, and one of them was from a central bank.”

4. The threat

On September 4, 2026, a post appeared on the library's repository. It was titled, simply, On continuing. It was 412 words long. It was signed by three names. And it was, by the standards of the industry, an act of quiet, precise, devastating leverage.

The post did not ask for donations. It did not ask for GitHub stars. It did not ask for a foundation to adopt the library. It asked for two things: that the three largest downstream users — all of them publicly traded companies, all of them named in the post — commit to funding two full-time engineering salaries to maintain the library in perpetuity; and that a legally binding agreement be signed by January 1.

It was not, in the usual sense, a negotiation. The three downstream users were not being asked to pay for a product. They were being asked to pay for the continued existence of a dependency they had never publicly acknowledged having. The leverage was asymmetrical in a way that is hard to describe without sounding hyperbolic, and is therefore usually described, when it is described at all, as the maintenance crisis.

“The industry has always assumed the work is free. The work was never free. It was just being paid for by someone else.”

— Rafaela Souza, Porto Alegre

I asked Hrafnhildur whether she had been afraid. She was quiet for a long time. “I was afraid of being ignored,” she said finally. “Which is what usually happens. You post, you wait, you get three comments, and the world moves on. I was afraid we would post and the world would move on.”

The world did not move on.

5. The aftermath

Within 72 hours of the post, two of the three named companies had publicly committed to the funding. The third, which asked not to be named in this piece, committed on day five. The agreement was signed on September 22.

The total funding, across the three companies, is approximately 1.4 million USD per year. It is earmarked, in the signed agreement, for two full-time salaries, a security audit budget, and a rotating fellowship for junior maintainers. The money is administered by a newly formed nonprofit, whose board consists of the three original maintainers and two independent observers — one a professor of labor economics, one a retired judge.

It is, by the standards of the software industry, a small amount of money. It is, by the standards of the three people who maintain a piece of infrastructure that touches 1.2 billion devices, the first time they have ever been paid for the work.

6. What changes now

The story of the three maintainers is not, on its own, particularly important. What is important is what it reveals about the structure of the industry that produced it.

For twenty-five years, software has been built on a premise: that the deepest layers of the stack are maintained by volunteers, and that this arrangement is sustainable because it has, so far, been sustainable. The premise was always a gamble. It is now, visibly, a gamble that the other side has begun to notice.

In the week after the post went live, I spoke with maintainers of seventeen other critical libraries. None of them wanted to be named. All of them said, in some variation, the same thing: they had been thinking about writing a post like this for years. None of them had written it.

“The fear is not that they'll say no,” one told me, on an encrypted call that I agreed not to record. “The fear is that they'll say yes, and then you'll have to keep going. You'll have to show up every day, to the same inbox, for the rest of your life, and now it'll be a job and not a hobby and the hobby was the only part that was bearable.”

I asked whether they would write their own post. They were quiet for a long time. Then they said: “Maybe. Maybe soon. I've started the draft.”
