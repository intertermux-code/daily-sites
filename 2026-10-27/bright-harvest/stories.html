§ 01

The month the hours cut

Featured story · 1,100 words · told to our volunteer recorder, June 2026

André Whitfield · Riverside · line supervisor, 9 years

The mill cut my shift from forty hours to twenty-four in February, and the first thing I noticed wasn’t the money. It was that I stopped knowing what was for dinner. You stand in a kitchen at six with two kids behind you and the fridge is a fact you have to face.

My sister told me about the shelf on Kearney Street. I went at five past six in a work shirt, planning to be quick about it, and the woman at the counter asked me what my family likes instead of asking me what happened to me. She said the cavolo nero cooks like spinach but takes a minute longer, and she wrote it on a card because she thought I’d forget.

That was the turn. Not the food, exactly — the fact that nobody had to hear a speech. I came back the next Wednesday with the kids. My daughter picked the squash herself and carried it to the cart like it was hers, and it was, because that’s how it works there.

By April I was short two months of rent and paying the pantry’s shelf card forward to the family in my building who was worse off. When the hours came back in July I didn’t stop coming — I started taking the Saturday shift on the dock instead. Now I run it. Eleven years of line work means I can weigh a crate by eye, and the new volunteers trust the number because I say it out loud.

People still talk about food banks like it’s a bottom drawer you open when you’re desperate. It’s a logistics problem. Somebody grew the food, nobody bought it, and here I am carrying it to a hall four blocks from where it was cut. The shame is in the story people tell about it, not in the thing itself.

Recorded by Dana Oyelaran Dana sits at the intake desk on Thursdays and types whatever neighbours want said. Every story on this page was approved line-by-line by the person who told it.
