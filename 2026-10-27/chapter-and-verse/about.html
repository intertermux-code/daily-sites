We Kept the Hardware Shelves

When the hardware shop on the corner closed in 2010, its owner sold us the fixtures for less than the freight would have cost. We put nails in the wrong place, filled the gaps with books, and never entirely fixed the floor, which cants two degrees toward the window.

Why “Chapter & Verse”

To Quote Chapter and Verse Is to Be Accountable

The phrase comes from the margins of a Bible: give the chapter and the verse and your claim can be checked. We took it as a job description.

A bookseller who has read the book can tell you which chapter turns. So we read. Every title on the long table has been opened by a person whose name is on the card, and if we recommend something you hate, come back and say so — that is the part of the bargain we actually enjoy.

The other half of the name is quieter. A chapter is a room you can walk into; a verse is a line worth walking back for. Those are the two speeds of reading we try to keep a shop for.

Fourteen years, briefly

How the Shop Became This Shape

  • 2011

    Opening, with 2,400 books and no till

    Imogen Rhee left a college teaching job and signed a five-year lease on a room she could not afford, using the hardware shop’s pine shelving. Opening night brought thirty-one people and one escaped hamster, which is still named in the staff ledger.

  • 2013

    The tea counter

    A customer asked whether she could sit with a book for two hours. We put a kettle, a hot-plate and six stools where the paint mixer used to be. Two years later it became hojicha and a bench, and the complaints about loitering stopped entirely.

  • 2015

    The standing shelf

    Two doors down, the old Davis Square stair was demolished. We bought its banisters for ninety dollars and built the eighteen-foot oak shelf that still faces our classics out, spine forward, at their real heights.

  • 2017

    Twelve steps down: the back room

    Theo Vance joined to run rare and antiquarian stock and dug out the cellar as a humidity-controlled room. Our first Jane Eyre three-volume set arrived in a laundry basket, misfiled by a previous owner as “Bronte, C. — misc.”

  • 2020

    Four hundred parcels on the pavement

    Closed for eleven weeks, we shelved books on the sidewalk and ran an honour ledger by message. Nobody stole anything; three people left money we had not asked for. The lending bench outside still carries the same chalk rules.

  • 2022

    Six clubs, one clipboard

    The Long Table outgrew the room, then the century, so we capped every club at twelve chairs. Waiting lists became offers, and offers became a rule we still keep: two absences pass the chair on.

  • 2026

    Now

    Twelve thousand four hundred titles, four of us behind the counter, forty event nights a year and one lease renewed to 2033. The floor still cants two degrees toward the window. We have decided to leave it.

    April: The Great Reshelve

    Every shelf comes out, every book goes back, and the poetry corner moves to the window because the light is better by then. Eleven days of ladder work, and the only week we close early.

    July: The Sidewalk Sale

    市

    Three trestle tables, one box marked Found in the Shop, and a card table where customers sell back what they finished. Last year the box produced a 1943 issue of a Japanese literary magazine nobody has claimed.

  • 秋

    October: The Long Read

    読

    Nine hours of Leaves of Grass aloud, twenty-two readers, one broken microphone. The shop shuts to browsing at four and reopens to anybody in the street.

  • 冬

    December: Candles and Stacks

    燭

    Overheads off on the second Friday, eighteen beeswax candles, and the back room opened so people can read first editions standing up. Mulled cider on the counter, one cellist from nine.

Behind the counter

Four People, and Whom to Ask

Ask for any of us by name. If we have read it, we will tell you; if we have not, we will say that too.

Imogen Rhee

Founder & owner · Fiction

Opened the shop in 2011 and still sets the long table herself. Reads two nineteenth-century novels a month and one new one, which she resentfully admits she sometimes likes.

Theo Vance

Rare books & the back room

Trained on a country-house library clearance and cannot resist an unfinished catalogue. Keeps the cellar at 60 degrees and argues for Marcus Aurelius in pencil.

Sadie Nakamura

Events, poetry & the young stacks

Runs the forty nights, times every reader from the counter, and teaches the children’s hour with a paper-crane method she refuses to improve.

Marek Dvořák

Counter, deliveries & repairs

Joining three days a week, and the only one of us who can re-sew a text block. If your book arrives glued and upright, that was Marek.

Down twelve steps

The Back Room, and Why It Is Cooler

Antiquarian stock needs steadier air than a New England shop can hold upstairs, so the cellar got the money instead of a new till.

Four shelves, a dehumidifier that runs from October, and the trade catalogues from the last sixty years in a box we call “the arguments.” Every rare book leaves with a provenance card, including the ones that were, honestly, bought in a laundry basket.

Five rules, on the wall by the till

What We Keep

一

Read What You Recommend

A card with no reader behind it goes in the bin. It is the only rule we have ever enforced with a rubber stamp.

二

Nothing Is “Just” a Classic

We shelve Genji beside its four translations and let you pick, rather than deciding for you by what looks scholarly.

三

Sell Nobody Time

No algorithm, no “customers also bought,” no email that is not one letter a month. You may sit here for three hours buying nothing.

四

Repair Before Replace

Marek re-sews; we reback; we tell you when a book is worth owning twice. Paperbacks on the shelf are not a failure of the hardcover.

五

Keep the Room Quiet Enough

Two degrees of cant in the floor, one kettle, no music before noon. The shop is not a café that happens to sell books.
