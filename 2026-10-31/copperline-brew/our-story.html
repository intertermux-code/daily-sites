Chapter One · 914 Ridge Road

We Bought a Fire That Had Gone Out

In 2010 the engine house on Ridge Road had been empty for six years, its copper hose-line stripped by scavengers and its drying loft full of pigeons. We bought the copper back, then built a brewery underneath it.

Fragments, Not a Brochure

What the Building
Remembered

Ten years of a brewery does not fit in a paragraph. So here is the wall: the smoke damage we left on purpose, the first pour, the batch we dumped on the sidewalk.

“The first batch we brewed was also the first thing we ever made together. It was fine. It was not good.”

Marge Kestrel · Founding Brewer

Six Years Empty

Engine 4 was disbanded in 2004. The apparatus floor stayed locked, the doors stayed painted, and the hose tower stayed exactly where the last crew left it.

House Rule No. 1

Pour It Like You’d Drink It

Lines flushed every morning, glasses never touched on the rim, and nobody on staff serves a pint they would send back. That is the entire quality manual.

7 bbl

Maximum batch size, and it will not grow

“We left the scorch mark above the bar. It came with the building.”

Dov Amaya · Co-Owner

The Sidewalk Batch

In August 2013 an infection got into fermenter three and we dumped 21 barrels into the storm drain on Ridge Road. A neighbour filmed it. The clip still gets found every few months, and we still get asked about it most Fridays.

Barrels lost · the year we learned to test pH

2010 to Now

Fifteen Years, Compressed

2010

The Auction Sheet

Marge Kestrel, Dov Amaya, and Priya Rangan buy the old Engine 4 house for more than they should have, financed by a home equity line and a brew-system loan that took nine months to clear. The building came with a hose tower and no plumbing worth keeping.

2011

First Pour, Long Bar

Seven-barrel system installed through a hole cut in the north wall, because the doors were too low. We opened on a Friday with three beers, forty stools built from the original locker timber, and a licensing officer who nearly turned around twice.

2013

The Year We Dumped Everything

A lactobacillus infection we could not find for six weeks cost us 21 barrels and most of our working capital. We rebuilt the cold side, started labelling every tank with a pH reading, and never once let a batch go out the gate unchecked again.

2016

Cellar and Canning Line

Six fermenters go in below the drying loft. A secondhand canner arrives on a flatbed and takes the crew four days to calibrate. Hook & Ladder becomes the first Copperline beer sold outside the building.

2019

Barrels, at Last

Forty bourbon barrels from a Kentucky rye program, hauled in on two trips. The first Second Alarm goes into them in November and comes out fourteen months later, which is the entire reason our release calendar looks the way it does.

2022

The Kitchen Happens

Chef Nuri Oyelaran joins part-time and stays full-time. Six things on the menu, one plancha, and a rule that nothing arrives on a plate that would fight a pint of amber ale.

2026

Still Seven Barrels

Nine staff, twelve lines, 41,000 pints last year, and one offer to scale up that we turned down in a hallway, in about four seconds.

Nine People, One Shift Rotation

Who Is Actually
in the Building

Nobody here has a title they did not earn by doing the work. If you ask who poured your beer, someone will tell you.

Marge Kestrel

Founding Brewer & Owner

Twelve years in production before she ever owned anything. Writes every recipe by hand, keeps the notebooks in a drawer nobody is allowed to tidy.

Dov Amaya

Co-Owner · Front of House

Built the long bar with a borrowed table saw. Runs service, the Friday crowd, and the argument about whether the patio heater count is enough.

Nuri Oyelaran

Chef · Six Things, Well

Left a fine-dining kitchen for one reason: she wanted a menu that pairs with stout. Her chocolate pot de crème is the reason we brew Night Watch.

Non-Negotiables

Four Things We Won’t Change
for Growth

Seven Barrels, Period

A batch you can taste in one afternoon is a batch you can stand behind. We have turned down three distributors and one very good offer.

Sourced Inside the State

Michigan malt from a farm thirty-eight miles north, hops from the Leelanau peninsula, water from our own softening loop. The exceptions are barrel and wheat.

The Room Is the Product

We spend our marketing budget on stools, heaters, and a sound system nobody complains about. That is the whole strategy.

Skip the Tips, Keep the Trust

Grain left over goes to a farm in Fowlerville. Spent yeast goes back into bread at two neighbourhood bakeries. Nothing in this building becomes landfill if we can help it.

Names Before Numbers

Every beer is named for something in this building before it is named for a marketing angle. It keeps the list honest and the labels weird.

Dates on Every Can

Pack date on the seam, best-by on the lid, printed by us. Any beer older than ninety days goes back into the kitchen, not onto a shelf.
