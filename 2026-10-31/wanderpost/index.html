The house position

We stopped counting countries and started counting days.

Speed is expensive. It costs you the second morning, the market you revisit, the baker who finally remembers your name.

Wanderpost began in a flat above a bookmaker's on Cowgate with a stubborn idea: a trip should be long enough to become boring in the good way — the way where you stop performing tourism and start living somewhere for a fortnight. We have written eleven guidebooks' worth of route notes on four places since 2011, and we have added a fifth exactly once, when the sixth one earned it.

Every journey below is walked, cooked in, ridden and waited out by our own guides before it is offered to you. If a route has a bad Tuesday in it, the route notes tell you about the bad Tuesday. That is the whole product, honestly: candour, plus enough time.

  • One route at a time. Our guides live on their route for months, not visit it quarterly.
  • Nine travellers maximum, including parties booking the whole table.
  • Two weeks is our floor. We have never sold a shorter trip and never will.
  • Rail, ferry, foot, bicycle. Internal flights are the exception we justify in writing.

Why travellers stay with us

Four reasons the reviews read like letters.

We are not the cheapest way to see Japan, and we would be a poor choice if you want a country in eight days. Here is what we are genuinely good at.

01 — Pace

Seventy-two hours in one city before we move on.

Our itineraries are built around the fact that a place opens on the fourth day, not the first. In Kyoto you will spend a full week in the same neighbourhood and be handed a library card at a branch library, which sounds like nothing until you have watched a room full of strangers become a room.

72 hrsMinimum before the next train

02 — People

Nine seats, and the same guide from arrival to departure.

No handovers, no coach drivers reading a script. Your guide cooks the breakfast, knows which clinic still takes English speakers, and has already argued with the ferry company about the 07:40. You get one phone number for fourteen weeks after you go home.

9 travellersPer departure · 1 guide

03 — Money

One price, paid here, with the receipts on the table.

We publish what a day of your money actually buys: guide pay, guesthouse, fuel, the fishmonger who teaches the knife session. Two suppliers in Patagonia are on three-year contracts with us because we paid a deposit when nobody else would.

61%Of your fare stays in the region

04 — The post

We still send things through the actual mail.

Two weeks before you travel, a brown envelope arrives: route notes, a paper map, a letter from your guide, and a stamp from the place you are going. It is the only part of the company that has never moved online, and the part travellers mention most often.

How a trip gets built

From first letter to the airport gate — five stages, no rush.

Enquiries open roughly nine months before each departure. We answer every one, even the ones we cannot take.

  1. 01

    You write to us

    A short form, five questions. We reply within two working days with a guide's honest read on the season you picked — including the years we would not go.

  2. 02

    We call, thirty minutes, no script

    What you have done before, what bores you, whether you want to be looked after or left alone on the fourth morning. Most trips are decided in this call.

  3. 03

    The route note lands in your postbox

    Forty pages, printed, ring-bound: day-by-day plan, packing list, the local word for “the good one”, the map with our hand-drawn cross on the café.

  4. 04

    You travel

    Flights booked through our ATOL-held arm, ground costs fixed. Two rest days per fortnight are built in and are not for sightseeing.

  5. 05

    You send us the postcard

    We ask for one photograph and one paragraph afterward. The best go on the stories page, and the honest criticism goes straight into next year's route notes.
