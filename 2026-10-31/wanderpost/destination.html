Kyoto, Month by Month — Including the Two Months We Refuse to Sell.

Fifteen years of route notes, condensed into one honest question: when should you actually go?

Updated 04 October 2026 Data from our own 61 departures Written by Aiko Nakamura

The city, plainly

A Grid, a River, and Hills on Three Sides.

Kyoto is not one city. It is a flat grid of an old capital with water running through it and a ring of forested hills that decide the weather you get.

Everything readable is between the Kamo river in the east and Nishijin in the west, with the Kyoto Imperial Park as the northern anchor and TOJI in the south. If someone tells you a temple is “in Kyoto” and it takes more than forty minutes by bus, they are describing a different city with the same postcode.

The useful fact for a first-timer: the grid is numbered. Streets run north–south and avenues run east–west, so an address like “Kamigyo-ku, Horikawa–Imadegawa” is a corner, not a riddle. Taxis understand corners. Your phone rarely does. We will teach you to read three of them on day one, which is more than most visitors need.

Second useful fact: the hills make the temperature lie. The north suburbs run two to three degrees colder than the centre in January and February, and Arashiyama in August is a sauna with a river in it. Our route is built in Kamigyo partly because the light there at 07:00 from November to March is the best free thing in the city.

    Two honest warnings. July and August are not for sale. We stopped running Kyoto departures in the summer of 2019 after three consecutive heat advisories; if those are your only dates, take Lofoten in June or Marrakech in November instead. And the last ten days of November is the single busiest walking fortnight in Japan — beautiful, and we do mean for you to come, but the Daimonji-view slopes fill at 16:30.

    Where to base yourself

    Five Neighbourhoods, and We Only Use Two.

    Choosing a district in Kyoto is choosing a set of sounds at 07:00. Here is what each one actually gives you.

    Our base

    Kamigyo — North Centre

    Timber lanes, the imperial park, and the Nishi market that locals still use. Ten minutes on foot to Kitano Tenmangu, and the 205 bus to Kinkaku-ji before the coaches arrive. This is where we live for twenty-one days, and where you should too if you want a trip rather than a checklist.

    Day one

    Higashiyama — The Eastern Slope

    Every photograph you have ever seen of old Kyoto, on one steep street, and therefore the busiest square kilometre in the country. It is extraordinary at 06:40 and unpleasant at 11:00. Stay here only if you intend to be up early; the lanes are lovely at midnight and empty.

    Dawn work

    Arashiyama & Nishikyo

    The bamboo path, the monkey park, the moss gardens at Okochi, and a river wide enough to sit beside. Twenty-two minutes on the Randen tram from central Kyoto, and the whole district empties by 10:30 if you have done the dawn walk — which is the point of the second visit.

    Worth the bus

    Ohara — The Northern Valley

    An hour north over two mountain passes: Jingo-ji in fog, Sanzen-in's moss, yuba for lunch, and almost nobody. Our two quietest days of the route happen here. Roads are single-track in winter and the last bus leaves early, so we drive you.

    Evenings

    Fushimi & the South

    Sake cellars, the Senbon Torii at closing time, and a Saturday crowd of university students along the Kamo. The famous thousand gates are a 05:45 or 17:00 affair; in between it is a queue on a mountain stair.

    Eating and sleeping

    What You Will Actually Be Fed, and Where You Will Sleep.

    Stay
    Machiya

    Twenty-one nights in one townhouse

    Our house is a restored machiya built in 1904, rebuilt in 2016 with the fire regulations respected and the original beams kept. Six bedrooms, three bathrooms, one long kitchen table that seats nine and is where most of the trip actually happens. Ground floor room is step-free. Twin, double and single are all available; single supplement is £0 when we can pair you, which we usually can.

    • Real futons on tatami in two rooms; proper beds in four
    • Laundry on day eight and day fifteen, folded and returned
    • No minibar, one very good kettle, and a bath you queue for politely

    Breakfast, lunch, dinner

    Breakfast is at home or at Nishi Market — rice, miso, an egg done in a way you will attempt at your own kitchen table in March. Lunch is the informal meal: soba, yuba, the tamago sandwich that four previous groups have fought over. Dinner happens eleven times, seven of them at counters with eight seats or fewer, and one night you will cook what you learned.

    Dietary needs: we handle vegetarian, vegan, gluten-free and halal on this route as standard — Kyoto has the best vegetarian temple cuisine tradition on the planet, so tell us and we will use it rather than work around it. Coeliac in the noodle districts requires care; we bring you a written card in Japanese.

    Money, cards and the coin problem

    Kyoto runs on cash more than Tokyo does. Carry ¥30,000 for the first week; the 7-Eleven ATMs take every British card and charge least. Temples want coins in a box, and a five-yen coin means “good connection”, which is why everyone buys them. Splitting a bill at a counter is not done — one person pays, and you settle it on the bus.

    Do this, not that

    Six Things That Decide How Kyoto Treats You.

    None of this is about offending anyone. It is about being readable, which is what earns you the second conversation.

    Do

    • Step off the pavement for the delivery bikes. They will not slow down, and they are right of way.
    • Say arigatō gozaimasu with the last three syllables. It takes one extra second and changes a face.
    • Photograph the lane, not the person. If a geiko is walking to work, she is working.
    • Stand on the left side of the escalator at Kyoto Station. The right is the fast lane and locals will use it.

    Not

    • Do not eat while walking in the temple districts — it is a specific, enforced no, and you will be asked to stop.
    • Do not tip. Nobody is rude about the refusal, but it is refused, and the confusion costs more than the money.
    • Do not talk on the bus after 20:00. Not a rule with a sign, just the city's agreement.
    • Do not book the Arashiyama bamboo path for midday in April. We are begging you.

    Getting Around, Without a Car.

    The subway is two lines and rarely the answer. The bus network is the real system: ¥230 flat, day pass ¥1,100 and worth it only if you cross three districts. Our route uses the 205 and 12 from Kamigyo, both of which fill by 09:15. Taxis are clean, expensive and they will not take a group of five — book two, or read the lane names aloud and the driver will know the corner.

    Bicycles change everything in the flat centre and nothing in the hills. Kamigyo to the imperial park is eight minutes on two wheels, and we hire them for the first week because the traffic on Horikawa is a road you cross rather than ride.

    Asked constantly

    Questions We Answer Before the Phone Rings Twice.

    Do I need to speak Japanese?

    No. The route works entirely in English with Aiko translating the moments that matter — the tea keeper, the knife shop, the market fishmonger. You will learn to order, and on day twelve you will do it alone at the counter, which is the only language lesson we require.

    Is twenty-one days too long for one city?

    It is the correct length and people worry about it on the phone until day six. The trip is not sightseeing every hour: two rest days, one library afternoon a week, and a standing lunch table. If you genuinely want more temples, we will build you a Nara or Kiso extension.

    Which month has the best weather with the fewest people?

    Late February or the second half of May. April is lovely and full; November is extraordinary and fuller. June is wet and cheap and we love the hydrangeas enough to run it anyway.

    Can I bring a child?

    Yes, from age nine, and we have run the route with three families. The knife session and the market are the hits; the tea lesson is where a nine-year-old will test the room's patience, and the room has plenty of it. Children pay the same fare because the guide ratio does not change.

    What happens if there is an earthquake or a typhoon?

    We hold a written plan for both, updated each April, and every traveller receives it in the envelope before they fly. Kyoto's shaking risk is lower than Tokyo's; our machiya was rebuilt to 2016 seismic code. Typhoon week in June and September gets the indoor craft days we keep in reserve.

    Is the machiya cold in February?

    The bedrooms are warm and the corridor is not. You are given a down jacket for the house, which sounds like a joke until you have worn one indoors at 07:00. The bath is the size of a good idea and everyone queues politely.
