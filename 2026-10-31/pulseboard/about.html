Origin // how this started

A checkout failure, a whiteboard, and a rule we still keep.

2019: the night the numbers disagreed

Our founders, Sofie Greveling and Daniel Okonjo, were running platform and product analytics at a European retailer. On the night of a big checkout migration, three dashboards reported three different conversion rates, and the on-call engineer had to pick one to believe. The migration got rolled back at 02:40 by feel, not evidence.

The post-mortem had one line that stuck: nobody trusted the instruments, so nobody could fly the plane.

2020: a panel bolted to the incident channel

They built an internal board that pulled from a single metric definition and repainted itself in the incident channel. It was ugly and it worked. Two things from that prototype survive: the tail window that streams rather than batches, and the rule that every number on screen names the definition it came from.

The first external users were other on-call engineers from the same retail group, who asked to point it at their own warehouses.

2021–2023: from tool to telemetry OS

Pulseboard became a company of eight in a rented loft above a bike shop. We added the versioned metric tree after watching a startup argue for four weeks about what “activated” meant. We added learned alarm bands after a customer retired eleven useless alerts in one afternoon and started sleeping.

By 2023 we had paying workspaces in fourteen countries and a rule we still enforce: no revenue from a plan a customer cannot explain to their own CFO.

2026: what we optimise for now

Today the fleet carries 2.4 B events a day for 1,284 workspaces. The company is deliberately small: 34 people, two offices, four on-call engineers who all still take turns on the pager. We measure ourselves on one internal metric — median time from a metric moving to a human deciding something about it.

Deck log // seven years, one instrument

Milestones, in the order they hurt.

Each entry is the thing we had to build because a customer could not get the answer they needed.

  • 2019

    Founded above a bike shop in Rotterdam

    Two founders, one prototype board, and a warehouse connector written in a weekend. First design partners came from the same retail group, under a one-page agreement.

  • 2020

    Streaming tail shipped; batch quietly retired

    We moved the default from nightly rollups to a live tail window. On-call engineers stopped asking “is this number refreshed?” — the question that had started all of this.

  • 2021

    The metric tree, version one

    Definitions became composable and versioned after a four-week internal argument at a customer about a single activation number. Impact preview came eleven months later.

  • 2022

    Toronto office opens; first 50 workspaces

    Three engineers and a support lead joined in Canada so the Americas got business-hours coverage. SOC 2 Type II audit completed in the same quarter.

  • 2023

    Learned alarm bands replace static thresholds

    Per-weekday and per-hour baselines cut fleet alert volume by 62% in the first month. The alert body started shipping with its own segment breakdown attached.

  • 2024

    Warehouse push-down for regulated buyers

    Banks and health networks could run Pulseboard definitions against their own tables. Data left nobody’s warehouse, and the console still painted in under half a second.

  • 2025

    Series A of €14 M, taken on our terms

    Funded to grow ingest capacity and residency regions, with the constraint written into the round: the fair-use and pricing rules stay unchanged and self-published.

  • 2026

    1,284 workspaces and the cohort ledger

    Cohorts anchored to arrival day with payback and expansion columns. Our own Monday stand-up now runs off the same board our customers read.

Standing orders // how we work

Four rules that survive reorgs.

They sound obvious. Each one exists because we broke it once and paid for the lesson in a customer incident.

01

One number, one definition

Every figure on screen links back to the metric that produced it. If two boards disagree, that is a bug, not a discussion.

02

Latency is a feature

A correct number that arrives after the decision was made is wrong. We budget milliseconds like other companies budget headcount.

03

Publish the awkward terms

Fair-use ceilings, credit formulas, and the sub-processor list live on this site. Nobody should need a sales call to read a limit.

04

Builders carry the pager

Everyone who writes ingest, modelling, or query code takes a turn on rotation. It keeps the alarms honest and the roadmap short.

Open berths // four roles hiring now

We hire for judgement, then hand you the pager.

Hiring is slow and written. Every role below ships something specific in the first quarter, and the interview loop is three sessions plus a paid work sample. Remote within Europe or Canada, four days a week in the office for the first six months.

Senior Query Engineer · Rotterdam

Own the tail-window execution path. You will spend the first quarter cutting p95 paint from 400 ms to 250 ms on boards above 60 tiles, and you will carry the pager for it.

  • Rust or C++ at the plan level, SQL as a second language
  • Comfort reading flame graphs to a room of non-engineers

Detection Engineer · Toronto

Improve how baselines learn weekday, hour, and campaign shape — and make every alert earn its place. You will delete more rules than you add in the first month.

  • Statistics with real data: seasonality, counts, thin segments
  • Python or Go, plus a healthy distrust of noisy paging

Solutions Engineer · Remote (CET)

Run the demo calls, rebuild a customer’s messy metric in front of them, and own the migration playbook. This role writes the feedback that sets the modelling roadmap.

  • Fluent in dbt, warehouses, and the politics of one shared number
  • Comfortable saying “that alarm will wake you up wrongly” and meaning it

Documentation & Product Writer · Rotterdam

Own the console guide, the terms page, and the migration handbook. Writers here ship into the product UI, not a separate wiki nobody reads.

  • You have rewritten a legal or billing page until it was short
  • Git, Markdown, and an opinion about sentence length
