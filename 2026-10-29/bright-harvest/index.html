There is more than enough food. We make sure it reaches EVERYONE

Every morning our vans leave Millrace Avenue with broccoli, bread, milk and produce that was headed for the compost. By dinnertime it is on family tables in 41 towns. No income forms at the door, no waiting lists, no questions about why you are here.

Five chapters, one pallet

Follow a Pallet From Field to Front Door

Most food bank tours show you the finished box. This one shows you the whole walk — the 5 a.m. phone call, the cold loading dock, the four-hour sort, the porch where it ends. Scroll the exhibits sideways, like a diorama at the county fair.

01 / Field

A grower calls before sunrise

Thirty-one farms in the Cedar Valley ring our dispatch line when weather, contracts or cosmetic standards make a block un sellable. Tuesday it was 2,400 pounds of carrots with the wrong shoulder — perfect carrots, wrong shape.

  • 31 partner farms
  • Dispatch 4:45 a.m.
02 / Road

Two refrigerated vans, four routes

Our drivers pick up grocery overstock and bakery seconds on the same loop, so nothing sits in the sun. Cold chain is the whole game: food rescued at 6 a.m. and chilled still has three good days on the shelf by the time a neighbor reaches for it.

  • 38,400 miles a month
  • Held at 34°F
03 / Dock

Forty people sort it by 11 a.m.

Warehouse volunteers break the pallet down into three streams: serve fresh today, can or freeze for later, or hand to our compost partner. Every item gets logged on a tablet, which is how we know a Tuesday in October needs more milk and fewer apples.

  • 4-hour shift
  • 96% kept edible
04 / Table

Packed for real dinners

A senior box is built around one-tap cooking and low sodium. A kid backpack is built around a Friday afternoon and a Monday morning. A family grocery order is built around whatever the household said they actually eat — because a recipe nobody uses is just food waste with good intentions.

  • 7 packing lines
  • Menu cards included
05 / Porch

It ends at a kitchen table

Fifteen pantries, nine school sites, four community fridges and two Mobile Market trucks. Neighbors shop with a coupon that spends like cash, picks their own produce, and leaves. Last count: 89% of the people who shop a Mobile Market return within the month.

  • 30 distribution sites
  • Dignity first

Seven Programs, One Standard

From the neighborhood

“I Was Not the Kind of Person Who Did This”

Dana Ferrell lost two shifts at the packaging plant in March and found herself rationing pantry pasta by the handful. A school counselor quietly signed her youngest up for the backpack program. By June she was shopping the Mobile Market in her own courtyard, picking tomatoes she said no. Eleven months later she greets people at that same truck on Thursday afternoons.

  • Neighborhood grocery coupons spend like cash — you choose your own food.
  • One visit is enough. Nobody is tracked, timed or required to explain themselves.
  • Four out of five people we serve send a homegrown vegetable back to the dock.
