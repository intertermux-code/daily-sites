The Hidden Cost of a Hundred-Millisecond Promise

Every content delivery network still sells sub-100 ms delivery. Signal spent eleven weeks instrumenting 41 production workloads across six providers. The number holds for the accounts that can afford to measure it — and quietly breaks for everyone else.

Marta Ellison
Senior Correspondent, Infrastructure

Published 29 Oct 2026, 06:00 CET

14 min read

Probes · 2.1M requests

The marketing has not changed in four years. Six of the eleven delivery networks we approached still promise a hundred milliseconds to the first byte of a cached object, anywhere in the European Economic Area. Two of them put the figure in the contract as a service-level objective. The other four describe it in a datasheet, with a footnote about “typical performance.”

So we stopped asking and started measuring. Between 4 August and 20 October, Signal ran 2.1 million requests from 41 production workloads that volunteer organisations agreed to let us instrument — a municipal library union, three universities, twelve small publishers, nine logistics operators, and eleven software cooperatives. We sampled every ninety seconds, from inside their own edge caches and from the last mile outside them.

The headline result is uncomfortable but not mysterious: the median measured p95 across the fleet was 136 ms, not the advertised 100. The interesting finding is where the gap sat. Workloads on enterprise contracts landed within four milliseconds of the promise, and their providers published the dashboards to prove it. Workloads on entry-tier contracts averaged +37 ms, and nobody published anything at all.

01 — MethodThe Measurement Was Deliberately Boring

We used the same instrument at every site: a small probe container that fetches three objects of different sizes from the nearest point of presence, records DNS, TCP, TLS, and time-to-first-byte separately, and pushes signed samples to a bucket we control. Nothing about the workload changed. The probes read the same caches the real traffic reads.

  • Sample window: 4 August – 20 October 2026, eleven weeks, ninety-second cadence.
  • Volume: 2,118,440 requests, of which 4,011 failed and are counted as failures rather than retried away.
  • Geography: 41 workloads across 14 countries, all inside the EEA, none behind a provider-owned resolver.
  • Disclosure: providers were told we were measuring and were not told which accounts participated. Five of six asked what the methodology would be. One asked us not to publish per-alias numbers; we publish per-tier numbers instead.

We deliberately did not use a synthetic monitoring service. Synthetic probes measure the network a monitoring vendor has bought, not the network your users inherited. The difference showed up immediately: our last-mile figures ran 18–26 ms slower than the same sites’ public status pages reported on the same days.

02 — Where the Thirty-Seven Milliseconds Come FromThe Divergence Is a Routing Decision

Thirty-seven milliseconds sounds like trivia until you notice it is not noise. It is a stable, reproducible, tier-shaped offset that appears at the same moment every day and disappears when traffic is cheap.

Three mechanisms explain most of the spread, and all three are visible in configuration rather than in hardware.

Anycast population. Entry-tier accounts are advertised from fewer points of presence. Four of the workloads we measured resolved, on a Tuesday afternoon, to a presence 1,400 km further from the requester than the one their own enterprise customers used. That alone was worth 22 ms.

Cache admission policy. Small accounts get smaller cache quotas and more aggressive eviction. The library union saw its hottest object — a 340 KB stylesheet used by every reading room — evicted 41 times in eleven weeks. Each eviction meant a cold round trip to origin, and origin sat in a region chosen by price.

Tiered egress paths. Two providers route discounted traffic through a shared peering aggregate rather than a private one. Congestion at 20:00 local time cost those workloads a further 11 ms, and the effect was entirely predictable from the daily curve.

“We didn’t lose latency to bad hardware. We lost it to a pricing page.” Infrastructure lead, municipal library union

03 — The BillWho Pays for the Gap

The cost is not only experiential. Three of the twelve publishers reported that the divergence pushed them over a traffic-shaping threshold, triggering a per-gigabyte rate they had not budgeted for. One cooperative moved 9% of its annual hosting spend on re-egress it did not expect to buy.

Meanwhile the enterprise tier keeps improving. Median p95 on negotiated contracts fell 6 ms year over year, driven by cheaper origin shields and better prefetch. The gap between tiers is widening because the top tier is optimised for measurement, and the bottom tier is optimised for margin. Both are honest engineering goals. Only one of them appears in the datasheet.

  1. Publish the tail, not the promise. Three providers now show a p95 histogram per region. Ask for it before you sign, and ask for it again every renewal.
  2. Instrument your own last mile. A 60-euro probe and ninety seconds of cadence will tell you more than any status page.
  3. Read the eviction counters. If your hottest object evicts more than once a week, your cache quota is a marketing number, not a capacity one.
  4. Price the egress tail. Shaping thresholds are the quietest line item on a delivery contract and the first one to bite in an election year.

04 — AccountabilityA Number Nobody Has to Defend

When we asked providers to explain the divergence, the answers clustered into two shapes. Five described the datasheet figure as a median — a statistic that says almost nothing about the tail a reader actually waits on. One described it as a target measured “at the edge,” which, taken literally, is true: measured at the edge, from inside the edge, by the edge, about the edge.

Two of the six have since changed their documentation. That is the fastest response we have ever seen to a measurement story, and it tells you the original wording was never load-bearing.

05 — What NextWhat Part 2 Will Measure

The probes stay in place. In November we will publish the origin-shield half of this dataset: where the cold round trip goes, what it costs, and how often an eviction reaches a server that was chosen for the cheapest available power contract. We will also publish every sample, unrounded, with the workload pseudonymised and the region intact.

If you run a small workload on an entry-tier contract and want it instrumented, the desk will do it for free and will name you only if you ask. The library union and four of the cooperatives are already in the second cohort.
