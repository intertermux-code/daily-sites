I

Chapter One — The Fire

We light it at 7 AM and it doesn’t go out until the last plate leaves.

Everything you eat here has met flame. Not a diffuser, not a pilot light — a bed of white oak embers raked flat by hand, hot at one end, gentle at the other.

We built the room around the box: twelve feet of brick hearth, an adjustable grill chain, and a mantel hung with hooks for the heavy iron. When a cut needs patience it slides toward the cool end. When it needs violence it goes directly over the coals and comes off in ninety seconds.

The wood is seasoned eighteen months at a mill in Ottawa, then split and stacked in the cellar behind the dining room, where it also happens to keep the floor warm in January.

“Fire is the only cook in the building you can’t automate. You have to stand there and decide.”

Nadia Roque — Chef & Co-owner

III

Chapter Three — Method

Open a movement. Read how the fire is run.

Four shifts, one fire. Tap any heading to unfold it — the room stays quiet while the panel springs open.

Prefer to hear it from us in person? The Fire Bar seats eight and the kitchen talks. Request bar seating when you book.

Build the bed

Split oak goes in at 7 AM in a single column. We light at the top and let it collapse — a stack that falls slowly gives an even, long-lasting coal bed with no flame licking the food.

By 11 AM we have three zones: a searing bank on the right, a medium shelf in the middle, and a cool rest on the left for anything that needs an hour of patience.

Read the heat

No probes on the line. The grill cook holds a bare hand six inches over the bars and counts: four seconds is a sear, seven is a roast, twelve is a gentle bake. Every ticket on the pass is timed against that count.

It is the slowest way to run a kitchen and the only one that keeps a thin lamb chop and a whole squash honest on the same fire.

Cook the market

The truck arrives at 9:40 AM. Whatever is best that morning earns a place on the sheet; whatever is tired gets sent to the staff table. That is why the menu is reprinted weekly rather than reprinted annually.

Expect the protein to hold and the produce line to move. Ask your server what changed that week — they enjoy telling you.

Serve one seating

Twenty-eight tables, filled once. You are not rushed because the room is not turning. Dessert can arrive at nine-thirty and nobody will look at the clock.

Come at five for the quiet end of the evening, or at eight when the hearth is a trench of coals and the whole room glows orange.

IV

Chapter Four — The Cellar

Two hundred bins chosen to stand up to smoke.

Our list leans to Jura, Etna, and the old-vine reds of the Beaujolais hills — wines with enough grit to meet char without turning to jam.

Sommelière Ines Vargas pours by the glass from a decanter list that changes with the fire: chilled reds for the first hour, deeper bottles once the coals flatten. There is no corkage on Tuesdays, and the sake shelf is serious.
